# The Thesis of Immanent Authority, Absolute Ends, and Material Actuality

### A philosophical framework of human agency, axiomatic ethics, absolute purpose, natural resistance, and material arbitration

## Preamble: The Question of Legitimacy

Does the end justify the means?

Ordinarily, the question invites an examination of the moral character of the desired outcome, the permissibility of the actions undertaken to achieve it, and the consequences arising from their realization.

Yet this examination introduces a more fundamental question: by what authority are ethical principles established, and from where does the legitimacy to act upon them originate?

The practice of ethics in the material world requires that abstract principles become concrete actions. These actions inevitably encounter other individuals, competing purposes, conflicting convictions, material limitations, and consequences that may be neither universally desired nor universally accepted.

The question is therefore not merely whether an end justifies its means, but whether human beings require an external moral authority to establish the legitimacy of their purposes and undertake their realization.

This thesis proposes that they do not.

Authority originates in human agency itself. Individuals and collectives establish purposes, formulate foundational principles, and pursue their realization without requiring authorization from an external moral guarantor.

An end, when established upon absolute and irreducible principles, provides its own internal foundation for justification. Its means derive their justification from the principles and purposes they serve, while remaining accountable to the axiomatic foundations that define the end.

Competing ends retain their respective claims to agency, resistance, and realization. Their interactions are ultimately subjected to material reality, which determines their consequences without issuing an objective moral judgment upon them.

The resulting philosophy rests upon three foundational propositions:

1. **Immanent authority:** Human agency is the origin of authority; no external moral authority is necessary to authorize human action.
2. **Absolute purpose:** An end established upon irreducible axioms provides the internal grounds for justifying its pursuit and the means through which it is realized.
3. **Material actuality:** The material world determines the consequences and realization of competing purposes without providing a universal moral verdict.

---

## I. Immanent Authority: The Origin of Legitimacy

Authority originates in human agency rather than in an external or transcendent guarantor.

Human beings do not require permission from a divine authority, universal moral tribunal, objective ethical order, or other conception of *l'Autre* to establish purposes, formulate principles, or act upon their convictions.

The ability to determine one's purposes and undertake their realization belongs to the condition of being an agent.

This does not mean that every exercise of power is necessarily legitimate in the eyes of everyone affected. Rather, it establishes that the origin of authority does not lie outside humanity.

An individual possesses the authority to establish and pursue their own ends. Individuals may associate, establish common purposes, and voluntarily delegate particular powers to collective institutions.

Such institutions derive their authority from the human agency through which they are constituted.

Delegation does not constitute the permanent alienation of the underlying agency from which authority originates. Nor can an institution acquire an inherently superior moral standing merely by virtue of its establishment.

Authority may be exercised collectively, but its origin remains human.

Consequently, no individual, institution, government, or collective possesses an automatic entitlement to universal obedience.

Those who reject an authority retain their own agency, their capacity to resist, and their capacity to establish alternative arrangements.

Legitimacy is therefore immanent to human existence and action rather than dependent upon an external moral authorization.

## II. The Absolute End

An absolute end is a purpose conceived in its fullest possible scope, without excluding relevant nuance, contingency, consequence, resistance, or competing interests.

It is not simply a desired outcome toward which one directs one's actions. It is a comprehensive conception of the reality being pursued, incorporating the conditions, sacrifices, opposition, and possible outcomes involved in its realization.

Nothing is excluded merely because it is inconvenient to the pursuit.

Even the suffering, resistance, autonomy, and competing purposes of those affected must be accounted for within the conception of the end.

The absoluteness of the end rests upon the completeness of its conception and the irreducibility of its foundational principles.

This does not necessitate omniscience concerning every practical circumstance. Human knowledge of the material world remains subject to discovery, interpretation, and correction.

An absolute end can therefore maintain its foundational identity while its adherents continue learning about the circumstances through which it must be realized.

The distinction is between uncertainty concerning implementation and uncertainty concerning the purpose itself.

An adherent may recognize incomplete knowledge, revise an interpretation, and reconsider a procedure without necessarily questioning the absolute character of the end.

So long as its foundational principles remain intact, its pursuit does not require external validation or universal approval.

## III. The Axiomatic Foundation

Every absolute end must rest upon foundational principles that establish its identity and govern its realization.

These principles are irreducible within the system they constitute and may be distinguished into two forms.

### A. Logical axioms

Logical axioms establish the foundational relationships upon which reasoning operates.

For example, within ordinary arithmetic, one plus one is equivalent to two.

Such a proposition functions as a foundational relationship within its formal system rather than as a conclusion requiring repeated external authorization.

To challenge it requires examining the underlying definitions, premises, or formal structure.

### B. Sacrosanct axioms

Sacrosanct axioms establish foundational values that cannot be exchanged, subordinated, or discarded without abandoning the principles upon which the end depends.

Their significance is not necessarily derived from their utility or contribution to another outcome. They possess foundational importance in themselves.

Consider liberty.

If liberty is established as an atomic principle, its existence possesses nonzero merit and cannot legitimately be assigned a value of zero merely because its preservation becomes inconvenient.

Its value is not conditional upon the quantity of other benefits that might be obtained through its surrender.

Even if sacrificing the liberty of everyone promised to preserve every other conceivable good, the deliberate surrender would remain a violation of the principle.

The distinction, however, must be made between surrender and defeat.

If liberty is destroyed through overwhelming external force after every possible effort at preservation has failed, this is a material consequence rather than a voluntary repudiation of the principle.

The principle may remain intact even where its adherents are defeated or its practical existence is extinguished.

An individual may reject an axiom, but their rejection does not retroactively invalidate it. It signifies that they have abandoned their adherence to it.

The validity of a foundational principle, within the system that establishes it, is therefore not dependent upon universal obedience.

## IV. The Justification of Means

The justification of means originates in the axiomatic structure of the absolute end.

An action is internally justified when it follows from the foundational principles of the end and serves the realization of its purpose.

It does not require universal acceptance, consensus, or authorization from those who disagree.

The means derive their justification from the end they serve.

However, this does not mean that every conceivable action becomes permissible simply by invoking an absolute purpose.

The foundational axioms remain binding. A means that directly contradicts an irreducible principle cannot be justified by appealing to the end, because doing so would undermine the very foundation from which its justification originates.

The ends and means must therefore remain internally coherent.

This establishes a distinction between absolute justification and unrestricted action.

An end may justify the means through which it is realized, but those means remain subject to the principles constituting the end.

Thus, the justification of action is internal to the purpose rather than dependent upon external moral authorization.

Two incompatible ends may consequently justify different and even mutually contradictory actions within their respective axiomatic systems.

Neither requires the other's approval to act.

## V. Adaptation Without Foundational Revision

Absolute purpose does not require inflexible implementation.

The foundational principles of an end remain unchanged, but its practical parameters, procedures, interpretations, priorities, and means may be revised.

New information may reveal previously unrecognized circumstances, consequences, or opportunities.

Such information may establish merit in accommodating opposition or changing the methods through which the end is pursued.

Resistance and disagreement may therefore serve as sources of information.

Where opposition demonstrates merit under the governing axioms, accommodation is appropriate. Parameters may be adjusted, procedures revised, and previously unanticipated circumstances incorporated.

Where opposition provides no such merit, its existence does not independently establish an obligation to revise the end or abandon its pursuit.

It remains an anticipated condition within the process of realization.

The distinction is between revising the implementation of a purpose and revising the principles that establish its identity.

An absolute end must be capable of learning without surrendering what makes it absolute.

## VI. Natural Resistance and the Plurality of Ends

No individual or collective possesses an exclusive right to demand universal acceptance of its purpose.

Human agency necessarily allows for the existence of differing and incompatible purposes.

Where one party pursues an end that another rejects, the latter retains its natural capacity to resist, oppose, appeal, and establish an alternative course of action.

Resistance is itself an exercise of agency.

Its existence does not automatically establish its correctness, just as the existence of an established authority does not automatically establish the legitimacy of its continued exercise.

Neither agreement nor disagreement provides a universal moral verdict.

The absence of consensus does not extinguish the right to act, nor does the assertion of an absolute purpose extinguish the right of others to resist it.

Each party may pursue its own ends according to its own foundational principles.

Their respective actions and competing claims must ultimately encounter material reality.

There is no presumption that incompatible purposes must reconcile, that competing principles must become commensurable, or that disagreement must produce a universally accepted conclusion.

Absoluteness is not equivalent to universality.

An end may be absolute to its adherents without becoming universally binding upon everyone else.

## VII. Liberty, Delegation, and the Charter

Liberty serves as an illustrative example of an atomic principle and of the relationship between individual agency and collective authority.

The right to liberty is not impartible or transferable as ownership.

Individuals may voluntarily delegate certain powers and responsibilities to others, but such delegation does not extinguish their underlying right of agency.

An individual may willingly obey, enter an agreement, recognize leadership, or participate in a collective hierarchy.

However, no such arrangement can permanently eliminate the individual's right to disobey.

Obedience remains a voluntary exercise of agency rather than an irrevocable surrender of it.

An individual may abandon their commitment to liberty, but this does not mean that liberty as a principle permits its own negation.

The individual's freedom to reject a principle must be distinguished from the principle's authority to authorize its own destruction.

### The collective charter

Individuals may voluntarily delegate certain powers for the common preservation of their liberty.

A charter of liberty may therefore be established as an instrument of collective agency.

It receives its authority through the individuals who constitute and uphold it. It does not possess an original sovereignty independent of them.

Its purpose is to protect the conditions under which individual liberty can be exercised.

It may consequently exercise protective authority against parties attempting to deprive individuals of those conditions.

However, its authority remains derivative.

It cannot legitimately place itself above the atomic principles from which its authority originates.

Opposition to the charter is not inherently opposition to liberty. Peaceful dissent, appeal, and advocacy for constitutional replacement must remain possible.

Should the charter abandon its foundational principles, individuals may exercise their natural right of rebellion and establish an opposing charter founded upon those same principles.

Such an opposing charter does not automatically possess moral superiority, but neither does the existing charter possess an inherent entitlement to permanence.

Both are expressions of human agency, and both must encounter the material reality of their competing claims.

An institution may defend the principles it represents, but it cannot claim exclusive ownership over them.

Thus, institutions remain instruments of human purposes rather than sovereign authorities over the individuals from whom their legitimacy originates.

## VIII. Competing Absolute Ends and Material Arbitration

Multiple parties may establish entirely incompatible absolute ends.

Each may possess its own irreducible axioms, internally coherent justification, procedures for adaptation, and conviction concerning the legitimacy of its pursuit.

Each may consider the actions of its adherents justified within its respective system.

Neither is required to surrender its foundational principles merely because another party disputes them.

Their conflict is ultimately expressed through material action.

The world arbitrates through actuality.

It determines what happens, what can be sustained, what is destroyed, what succeeds, and what fails.

However, material arbitration must not be confused with moral adjudication.

A party that prevails has established a material outcome, not necessarily a universal moral truth.

An end that fails to realize itself has encountered material defeat, not necessarily moral invalidity.

The world does not deliberate over competing human purposes. It imposes physical conditions and consequences without offering an objective moral opinion.

Its arbitration is therefore material rather than normative.

This distinction is central to the thesis.

The world's consequences may demonstrate that a particular method was ineffective, that a material assumption was mistaken, or that an intended outcome was unattainable.

They do not, by themselves, establish that the foundational principles of an end were morally incorrect.

## IX. Material Actuality and Rationality

The thesis recognizes the relationship between rational purpose and material actuality.

The maxim, "What is rational is actual, and what is actual is rational," is employed here in a restricted sense concerning the realization of rational purposes within material reality.

A purpose must encounter actual conditions to become realized.

Its conception alone does not guarantee its material existence.

Likewise, material actuality establishes the conditions and consequences against which the pursuit of a purpose must operate.

This does not mean that everything that exists is morally justified, or that whatever prevails is necessarily rational according to every ethical system.

Nor does it mean that material success proves the superiority of an end.

Rationality within an axiomatic system and material actuality are distinct considerations.

An end may be internally coherent yet materially unrealizable. An end may be realized while remaining incompatible with the principles of its opponents.

Material actuality establishes what has occurred, not an objective judgment of what ought to have occurred.

## X. The Absence of an External Moral Guarantor

There is no necessary universal moral authority standing beyond human agency that determines the legitimacy of every human purpose.

Moral judgments arise from the principles, values, and purposes through which human beings interpret and organize their existence.

Such judgments may be internally coherent, sincerely held, and absolute within their respective foundations without becoming universally binding.

The absence of an external moral guarantor does not mean that human beings cannot establish ethical principles or distinguish between actions according to them.

It means that the authority to formulate and practice ethics does not originate from an external source.

Human beings are simultaneously the authors of their purposes, the agents of their realization, and the participants in the material consequences that follow.

This position does not establish that every asserted purpose is objectively true, nor that every action is universally justified.

It establishes that external moral authorization is not a necessary precondition for action.

Three distinct considerations must therefore remain separate:

**Internal legitimacy:** The justification an action possesses within the axiomatic foundations of the end it serves.

**Human authority:** The authority originating in individual agency and expressed through voluntary collective association and delegation.

**Material actuality:** The consequences and outcomes arising from the realization of competing purposes.

These considerations interact, but none should be mistaken for another.

In particular, the existence of human authority does not establish universal authority over others, and the material success of an action does not establish its universal moral correctness.

---

## Conclusion: The Primacy of Human Agency and the Realization of Absolute Ends

The ends justify the means when their justification originates in the irreducible principles constituting the ends themselves.

An absolute end provides its own internal grounds for action. Its foundational axioms establish its identity, its procedures govern its implementation, and its adherents pursue its realization without requiring universal agreement or external moral authorization.

Its practical methods remain adaptable to new information and material circumstances, while its atomic principles remain unrevised lest the end be corrupted at its foundation.

Human authority originates in agency itself.

Individuals may establish purposes and pursue them independently. They may voluntarily associate, delegate powers, establish institutions, and collectively defend the principles they uphold.

Such institutions remain accountable to the human agency from which their authority originates. No delegation can permanently extinguish the underlying capacity for dissent, disobedience, resistance, or constitutional replacement.

Competing purposes retain their respective claims to action and resistance.

Their proponents need not reconcile their principles or obtain one another's approval. Each may pursue its own conception of the absolute while encountering the consequences imposed by the material world.

The world arbitrates through actuality, not through moral judgment. It determines what becomes realized, sustained, transformed, or destroyed, without establishing a universal verdict upon the purposes that produced those outcomes.

Thus, no external authority is necessary to grant humanity permission to act, no universal agreement is necessary to establish an absolute purpose, and no material victory is sufficient to establish universal moral superiority.

The distinction is between the authority through which human beings establish their ends, the principles through which they justify their actions, and the material reality in which their pursuits are realized.

**Human agency is the origin of authority. Absolute purpose is the foundation of internal justification. Irreducible axioms establish the limits of legitimate means within each end. Natural resistance preserves the plurality of human agency. Material actuality determines the consequences of their competing pursuits.**

The world does not owe humanity a moral verdict, and humanity does not require the world's moral authorization to pursue its purposes.

### Final proposition

**The legitimacy to establish and pursue an end originates in human agency, not in an external moral authority. An absolute end supplies its own axiomatic grounds for justifying its means, provided those means remain consistent with its irreducible principles. Competing ends retain their respective capacities for resistance and realization, while material reality determines their consequences without furnishing a universal moral judgment.**
