# Formalism and scientism

This record covers a working session on November 21, 2026. The session
started from the author's concern that the project's philosophical foundation
could slide into scientism, or into pseudoscience through loose use of
cybernetics and complex systems theory. It ended with a reading of the
project as engineering, and with one open question: which school of theory
the project can draw on without being captured by pseudoscience.

## Decisions

The author settled the following points:

- **The project is engineering, not academic research.** It doesn't seek new
  insight into the questions it touches. It tests whether social organization
  works better when it's formalized and governed through a model by
  quantitative or symbolic logic, instead of by semantics and human opinion.
- **The degree of formalism is a meta-optimality.** The structure is meant to
  be expressive enough that how much to formalize is an output of design, not
  a fixed stance. Judgment-based mechanisms, such as a panel or a vote, stay
  among the options that a charter can select.
- **Current organic structures are proto-scientific.** Institutions and the
  social science behind them are held as opinionated truths, not as
  strategies with conditions of success. Their position resembles natural
  philosophy before the scientific method. The project's aim is to turn
  these arrangements of means into engineered ones.
- **The end is normative, not scientific.** The Question will supply an end
  centered on the capabilities approach to liberty. The author offers it as a
  normative belief, not as a scientific claim. Conditioned preference is
  unavoidable, and opinion is a necessary part of the overall structure.
- **Measures are incentives by design.** The tendency to optimize toward a
  measure is part of what a charter designs, not a defect that the
  measure suffers from outside.
- **Data is the prospect for cheap experiments.** Large-scale data collection
  and processing make social experiments affordable in a way they weren't
  before. How to use that data is a separate question.
- **This material belongs in research notes.** It's the work behind the
  project, not a formal part of it, so it doesn't go into the glossary.

## The scale of formalism

The session described a scale. At one end, judgment decides everything. At
the other end, a model computes everything except three things that can't be
formalized:

- Ends, because someone has to set the objective
- Interpretation, because someone has to decide which events count as which
  inputs
- Revision, because someone has to decide when the model is wrong

Historical attempts near the formal end, such as Soviet planning, Project
Cybersyn, and the high-modernist states that James Scott describes, failed
on incentives, tacit knowledge, and measures that degraded once they became
targets. Those failures suggest that the best point lies inside the scale,
not at its end. The best point also moves: as measurement, computation, and
coordination get cheaper, it shifts toward the formal end. Today's
institutions likely sit short of it.

The author's meta-optimality answers the charge of scientism. Scientism
assumes that formal method wins. The Framework instead computes how much to
formalize for each charter, against that charter's objective. Choosing that
degree needs an objective, and the objective comes from members, so the
regress ends in human agency, where the
[thesis of immanent authority](../site/content/terms/thesis-of-immanent-authority.mdoc)
places authority.

The past attempts also formalized everything into one model with one
objective. The charter graph engineers control inside each charter and keeps
variation and selection between charters, through formation, competition,
and exit.

## Means, ends, and experiment

The analogy with natural science holds for means, not ends. Natural science
could set values aside. Society can't, because its objectives are contested.
The Framework takes ends as input, so a scientific method applies to how
society is organized relative to a stated end, never to which end is right.

The capabilities approach suits that role. Amartya Sen developed it partly
in response to *adaptive preferences*: deprived people learn to want little,
so a measure of satisfied preferences reads their deprivation as
satisfaction. The approach is normative, but indices such as the Human
Development Index already measure it, so it gives the engineering a target
without a claim that the end is scientific. Sen declined to fix a list of
capabilities, and Martha Nussbaum proposed one. That choice affects how much
of the end can be formalized.

Natural science grew once experiments became cheap and repeatable. Data
supplies measurement, but observation alone shows what happened, not what
would have happened under another design. The charter structure supplies the
other half: many small units that run different designs, close to Louis
Brandeis's "laboratories of democracy."

## Open questions

The following questions remain open:

- **A school of theory.** The author plans a thorough exploration of the
  territory held by cybernetics and complex systems, to delineate a school of
  theory that won't capture the project into pseudoscience. The Santa Fe
  Institute is the first candidate. This exploration also has to answer
  what keeps the project's own claims, such as decomposability, from becoming
  one more opinionated truth, and whether the subtitle keeps the term
  "Applied Sociocybernetics."
- **The edge of chaos.** The author named this as an explicit topic for the
  exploration. The *edge of chaos* is the claim that adaptive systems perform
  best, or evolve toward, a regime between order and disorder. It came out of
  work by Christopher Langton, Norman Packard, and Stuart Kauffman at the
  Santa Fe Institute. Later studies, such as Mitchell, Hraber, and Crutchfield
  (1993), failed to reproduce some of its early results. The exploration
  has to settle which parts hold up, and whether any of it bears on how far a
  charter should formalize.
- **The gap between measure and end.** Designing a measure as an incentive
  works when the measure covers what the end values. Holmström and Milgrom
  (1991) show that strong incentives on measured tasks pull effort away from
  unmeasured ones. Where a measure covers the end only partly, the design
  has to account for that gap.
- **Use of data.** How the project uses large-scale data for experiments is
  still open.
