# A higher charter without democratic intent

This document looks at one case: the lower charters are democratic, but the
higher charter above them wasn't set up with democratic intent. It finds where
the glossary already handles the case, where it assumes the case away, and
what the author has to decide.

Status as of October 25, 2026. Nothing in this document changes the glossary.
Each recommendation is a decision for the author.

## Why the case matters

The glossary treats democracy as a property of every charter. The
constitutional authority entry says that the power to create, interpret, and
amend a charter "belongs to the corporation's members." The fundamental
protections entry and the anti-capture protection entry both protect
"genuine democratic authority over charter evolution." Nothing in the
glossary says what happens when a charter above democratic charters doesn't
meet that standard.

The case isn't rare. It's the usual way that higher authorities form:

- States mostly formed through war and conquest, not through the consent of
  the communities they absorbed (Tilly, *Coercion, Capital, and European
  States*, 1990).
- Federations mostly formed as bargains between elites facing an outside
  threat (Riker, *Federalism*, 1964).
- Online communities that run themselves democratically usually sit under a
  platform that its owner governs alone. Schneider calls this pattern
  *implicit feudalism* (Schneider, "Admins, Mods, and Benevolent Dictators for
  Life," 2022).
- Democratic member states make many European Union decisions through their
  executives in the Council, which the literature calls the *democratic
  deficit* (Follesdal and Hix, 2006).

The Social Charter also meets the case at its own start. The author describes
the framework as a pluralist vanguard. Someone has to write the first higher
charter before any members exist to vote on it.

## Four ways the case arises

A higher charter can lack democratic intent in four different ways. Each one
fails at a different point, so each needs a different response.

| Route | How the higher charter forms | Where democracy fails | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Imposition | An outside power or faction takes control of a commons that the lower charters depend on, and claims to arbitrate | No consent at any point | Conquest, a platform owner, a resource chokepoint, a patron |
| Executive cartel | The executives or delegates of democratic lower charters found the parent among themselves | Members of the lower charters never approve the parent's terms | The EU Council, cartel parties (Katz and Mair, 1995), trade associations |
| Founder's charter | A vanguard designs the parent, then invites charters to join | The design comes before any members exist | The Social Charter's own start, Rousseau's lawgiver, the Philadelphia Convention |
| Faulty composition | Each level votes democratically, but the rule that combines votes across levels produces results that most individuals oppose | The outcome, not the intent | One charter, one vote. The referendum paradox (Anscombe, 1976; Ostrogorski paradox). |

A fifth route, drift, is out of scope here. A higher charter that starts out
democratic and then degenerates is a capture problem, which the anti-capture
protection entry and the vanguard interest premise already cover.

The author has already named one instance of imposition. In the review of
factional interest, the author said that a faction of principle either gains
influence over the whole commons, and so becomes the higher charter, or stays
subject to the charter without a voice. That route makes a higher charter out
of a faction that won the struggle, not out of a democratic decision.

## What the glossary already offers

Several existing entries limit the damage that a higher charter without
democratic intent can do.

- **Delegation.** "Delegation transfers the power to act. It doesn't transfer
  the power to redefine the interest being served." If a lower charter's link
  to its parent is a delegation, the parent can execute but can't redefine
  the lower charter's interest. A parent that isn't democratic is then closer
  to an executive agent than to a sovereign.
- **Exit.** A democratic lower charter can leave a parent that serves it
  badly.
- **Limit of arbitration.** Where no shared interest covers the parties, they
  settle the conflict through bounded struggle. An imposed authority with no
  shared interest falls under this mode, not under arbitration.
- **Charter graph.** A corporation can have several parents, so it isn't
  bound to one higher authority for every dimension of its interests.

## Where the glossary assumes the case away

Each protection in the preceding list has a gap when the parent isn't
democratic.

### The parent writes the exit terms

The exit entry defines exit as a right to leave "under its exit procedure,"
which means the procedure of the corporation being left. When a lower charter
leaves a parent, the parent wrote that procedure. The arbitration entry adds
that exit penalties are agreed before arbitration, but a parent that isn't
democratic sets the terms of that agreement. Exit protects members of a
charter from that charter. It doesn't yet protect a charter from its parent.

### Admission is reviewed in one direction only

The admission entry lets a parent review a newcomer's purpose, jurisdiction,
and compatibility. No entry lets the newcomer review the parent. Admission
means accepting the parent's charter as a higher-order constraint, but the
glossary doesn't say who inside the lower charter decides to accept it.

### The members of a parent are corporations

The parent corporation entry says that a parent's members are other
corporations. It doesn't say how a corporation acts as a member: through a
vote of its own members, through its executive, or through a delegate. If
executives act for their charters, then the members of every parent are a
small group of executives. The vanguard interest then appears again at every
level above the first, and each level adds one more step between individuals
and the decision. Michels predicted this pattern, and Dahl applied it to
international organizations ("Can International Organizations Be
Democratic?", 1999).

### Consent under dependency still looks like consent

A democratic lower charter can vote to join a parent that isn't democratic
because the parent controls a commons that the lower charter needs. The
meditations describe this pattern as the rentier trap and the intelligence
curse: a ruler that controls a concentrated resource doesn't need the
cooperation of the people below it. Hobbes drew the same line between a
*commonwealth by institution*, formed by mutual agreement, and a
*commonwealth by acquisition*, formed by submission to a power that already
holds control (*Leviathan*, 1651, chapters 17 and 20). Hobbes counted both
as valid. The Social Charter has to decide whether it does.

### The highest human charter has nothing above it

Below the top, a lower charter can escalate against a parent that isn't
democratic, or move to another parent. At the highest human charter, no
parent exists to escalate to. The only remedies are exit and struggle under
the Natural Charter. The review record already notes the same gap for exit
penalties at the top level.

### Composition doesn't preserve majority rule

Even with democratic intent at every level, the rule that combines lower
charters' decisions can produce an outcome that most individuals oppose. In
the referendum paradox, a proposal can win a majority of charters while
losing a majority of all their members. One charter, one vote also gives a
member of a small charter more weight than a member of a large one. The
power balancing entry rejects influence proportional to membership, so the
glossary allows this result on purpose. It doesn't say what limit, if any,
applies.

## Possible responses

The responses fall into two groups. Some change the framework's structure, so
they're decisions for the author. Others already have an established range of
options, so each charter's design chooses among them.

### Responses that change the framework

The following responses would each change the structure of the framework:

1. **Treat a higher charter without members' authority as outside the
   framework.** On this view, a parent needs constituents who share a
   higher-order common interest and who confer its authority. An imposed
   authority isn't a parent. It's a power that the lower charters relate to
   through struggle under the limit of arbitration, even if they cooperate
   with it for strategic reasons. This option keeps the glossary's democratic
   definitions intact and puts most of the cases from the earlier table under
   an existing mode.
1. **Treat joining a parent as an amendment of the lower charter.** Accepting
   a higher-order constraint changes the lower charter's constitution. If
   admission has to pass the lower charter's own amendment procedure, then a
   founder can draft a parent without democratic intent, but the parent gains
   authority over each constituent only through that constituent's members.
   The US Constitution followed this pattern: an unelected convention that
   exceeded its mandate drafted it, and conventions in each state ratified it
   (Madison, *Federalist No. 39*, 1788). This option answers the founder's
   charter route and the executive cartel route. It doesn't answer consent
   under dependency.
1. **Agree the exit terms before admission, and let both sides set them.**
   The arbitration entry already agrees exit penalties before arbitration.
   Moving that agreement to the moment of admission, and requiring the
   constituent's approval of it, closes the gap where the parent writes the
   exit procedure alone.
1. **Make democracy a design choice, not a definition.** The author has
   already declined a framework-level capability floor and a floor for
   non-members, to keep design flexible. The same reasoning could apply to
   democracy: a charter could choose a nondemocratic design, and members
   would join or leave knowing it. This option fits the pluralist vanguard,
   but it conflicts with the constitutional authority, fundamental
   protections, and anti-capture protection entries as now written.

Options 2 and 3 work together. Option 1 and option 4 point in opposite
directions, so the author probably has to choose between them.

### Responses left to charter design

The following choices already have established options, so the framework only
needs to state that each choice exists:

- How a lower charter acts as a member of its parent, ranging from an
  executive with full discretion, to an instructed delegate, to a vote of all
  its members
- Whether individuals also hold direct membership in the parent, as in a
  bicameral federation with a chamber of states and a chamber of citizens
- How the parent weighs its constituents, ranging from one charter, one vote,
  to weights proportional to membership, to degressive proportionality
- Whether the parent's charter expires and needs ratification again after a
  fixed term
- How a lower charter limits what it delegates, such as through revocable
  grants, enumerated powers, or sunset terms

## Effect on the premises

This case bears on two premises and one intent.

- **Vanguard interest (P2).** The framework answers P2 by letting members set
  the objective. In a parent whose members are corporations, that answer
  holds only if each corporation's own members decide how it acts as a
  member. Otherwise the vanguard returns at the parent level, one level
  removed. The earlier review found that the vanguard moves to mechanism
  designers and interpreters. This case adds a third place it can move to:
  the executives who act for charters inside their parents.
- **Question of arbitration (P1).** A parent without democratic intent can
  set a clear agenda quickly, which answers P1. Founders and imposed powers
  often succeed for this reason. The case shows the trade-off between P1 and
  P2 at the parent level, which is the same trade-off the framework exists to
  improve.
- **Pluralist vanguard.** The author's intent keeps a vanguard's coordination
  and directive advantages. A founder's charter is the most direct form of
  that intent. The framework needs a rule that lets a vanguard found a higher
  charter without letting it keep the authority it used to found it. Option 2
  is one such rule.

## Decisions for the author

The following decisions are framework-level, because no established range of
options settles them:

1. Is a higher charter that its members don't hold constitutional authority
   over still a charter in the framework, or is it an outside power that the
   lower charters relate to through struggle?
1. Does joining a parent count as an amendment of the lower charter, so that
   the lower charter's members have to ratify it under their own amendment
   procedure?
1. Who sets a constituent's exit terms from a parent: the parent, the
   constituent, or both, at the moment of admission?
1. Is democratic authority over a charter's evolution a framework
   requirement, or a design choice like the capability floor?
1. How do charters relate to an existing establishment above them that isn't
   a charter, such as a state? This decision is the practical form of the
   case for a movement that forms charters inside an existing state, and it
   connects to the leverage answer in the preface.

Decisions 1 and 4 depend on each other. If democracy is a definition, a
nondemocratic parent falls outside the framework. If democracy is a design
choice, the framework has to admit nondemocratic parents and protect lower
charters from them some other way, most likely through decisions 2 and 3.

## Answers from the initiation review

On October 27, 2026, the initiation and admission review answered several of
these decisions. See that document for the author's positions:

- Decision 1 is narrowed. A party that doesn't ratify a parent's formation
  isn't its member, and relates to it only through a status.
- Decision 2 is answered. Converting a contract into a parent needs each
  party's ratification.
- Decision 3 is answered. The contract sets exit terms jointly.
- Decision 4 stays open.
- Decision 5 is answered. Charters relate to an existing state through
  contracts and statuses, which can differ by commons.
