# Initiation and admission

This document looks at how a charter starts and how people and charters come
to belong to it. It covers three gaps that the author raised:

- The glossary has no account of how a charter is initiated, only a list of
  formation routes that assumes the founding act already happened.
- Support for a charter is never fully free. Material circumstances push
  people toward support or opposition regardless of their opinions.
- People don't divide into members and non-members. They divide at least into
  support, opposition, and apathy.

Status as of October 27, 2026. The author answered four rounds of decisions
on the same day, and this document records those answers. The answers are
integrated into the glossary, as the "Effect on the glossary" section
describes.

## The gaps

The glossary treats formation and admission as procedures. The charter
formation entry lists three routes: voluntary association, act of
commission, and independent formation followed by admission. The admission
entry describes a review by the parent. Neither entry says who acts before a
charter exists, what the joining party agrees to, or what happens to people
who neither join nor refuse.

### Initiation is done by a vanguard

Before a charter exists, it has no procedure for its members to deliberate.
So someone outside any charter procedure identifies the meeting of interests,
draws the boundary of the commons, and drafts the first articles. This is the
founding paradox: a people is needed to make good laws, and good laws are
needed to make a people (Rousseau, *The Social Contract*, 1762, book 2,
chapter 7. Honig, "Between Decision and Deliberation," 2007).

The paradox has two consequences for the Social Charter:

- The vanguard interest (P2) returns at the start. The core thesis turns a
  meeting of interests into a known game. Initiation decides which game. This
  is agenda-setting power, the "second face of power" (Bachrach and Baratz,
  1962).
- A charter needs a real commons, and a real commons exists before the charter
  that governs it. People who don't join can still claim it. So founding a
  charter over a shared commons is a claim made in struggle, not a
  cooperative act from their side.

### Support depends on material circumstances

A decision to join doesn't cleanly show will. A "yes" can mean any of the
following:

- The charter matches the member's material interest. The framework already
  treats this as legitimate, because negative factions form around material
  position.
- The member has no viable alternative. The undemocratic higher charter
  review calls this *consent under dependency*.
- The member's circumstances shaped what they want. Elster calls this an
  *adaptive preference* (*Sour Grapes*, 1983). Lukes calls the power to shape
  wants the "third dimension" of power (*Power: A Radical View*, 1974).

This determinism cuts two ways. For legitimacy, it means admission is a noisy
signal of will. For mechanism design, it helps: if material position largely
decides support, support is predictable, and predictable inputs are what the
calculability assumption (A4) needs.

### Apathy is a third position

Organizers already use a five-point *spectrum of allies*: active allies,
passive allies, neutrals, passive opponents, and active opponents. Rusbult
added *neglect* to Hirschman's exit, voice, and loyalty, and neglect is close
to what the author calls apathy (Rusbult and others, 1982. Hirschman, *Exit,
Voice, and Loyalty*, 1970).

Apathy connects to the material gap. People without time, money, or contacts
take part less (Verba, Schlozman, and Brady, *Voice and Equality*, 1995).
Under preference falsification, apparent apathy can also hide support or
opposition (Kuran, *Private Truths, Public Lies*, 1995, already cited in
meditation 1).

## Author's position

The author answered the three decisions from the first round.

### Initiation is a gradient between two forms

Initiation takes one of two forms at the ends of a gradient:

- **Voluntary admission.** Autonomously organized struggle establishes a
  charter where it stands. The charter then seeks admission into a parent.
- **Interventionist commission.** A parent organizes a subsidiary charter to
  establish itself on a jurisdiction. The parent then grants the subsidiary
  rights according to their mutual articles.

Most cases fall between the two ends. A transient *contract* sets the exact
composition and terms of each case. The two parties maintain the contract
from the period of establishment or commission until the period of admission
or enablement. The circumstances of each case shape the contract, and it's
optimized toward both parties.

### The contract absorbs material circumstances

The author's answer to the material question is the same contract. Material
circumstances are inputs that shape the contract's terms. They don't
invalidate consent.

### Five statuses

A charter relates to people and corporations through five statuses:

| Status | Relation to the charter |
|---|---|
| Member | Subject to the full articles of the charter |
| Associate | Maintains a contract with the charter |
| Neutral | Holds no contract and no stated opposition |
| Opponent | Opposes the charter, but the conflict is reconcilable |
| Enemy | Opposes the charter, and the conflict is unreconcilable. Enemies are included in the charter's set objectives. |

## Reassessment

The author's answers close most of the first round and connect to earlier
open decisions.

### What the answers settle

The answers settle the following points:

- **The founding paradox has a place in the framework.** The contract period
  makes initiation a provisional state with explicit terms, instead of an
  act outside the framework. A vanguard can still initiate, at the commission
  end, but its authority over the new charter is bounded by a contract that
  the other party also maintains.
- **The three formation routes become one gradient.** Independent formation
  followed by admission sits at the voluntary end. Act of commission sits at
  the interventionist end. Voluntary association under a parent sits between
  them.
- **The framework treats compelled and free admission alike, but makes the
  difference visible.** This position matches Hobbes, who counted both a
  commonwealth by institution and a commonwealth by acquisition as valid
  (*Leviathan*, 1651, chapters 17 and 20). It fits the author's decision to set
  no capability floor. It also fits the narrowed form of obscured consensus
  (P3): the contract makes the terms of the problem explicit, so the degree of
  dependency shows in the terms instead of hiding in side channels.
- **Apathy has a status.** Neutrals are the apathetic. The spectrum of allies
  suggests that neutrals are where a big tent grows, because organizers move
  people one step along the spectrum more often than they convert opponents.
- **Struggle has a status.** Opponents are the parties to struggle within the
  limit of arbitration, and enemies are the parties outside it. The enemy
  status matches the author's earlier example of a group too alien to
  include, and the earlier position that the coalition decides exclusion.

### Links to the undemocratic higher charter review

The contract bears on three of that review's open decisions:

- **Decision 2, joining as an amendment.** The contract is the vehicle for
  joining. Whether the contract needs the joining charter's own amendment
  procedure is still open.
- **Decision 3, who sets exit terms.** A contract optimized toward both
  parties implies that both sides set the terms. If the author confirms this
  reading, decision 3 is answered.
- **Decision 5, relations with an existing non-charter state.** A charter at
  the voluntary end that forms inside an existing state has no parent to make
  a contract with. The contract model might extend to the state as the other
  party, or the relation might stay pure struggle.

### What the answers leave open

The contract makes dependency explicit but doesn't reduce it. The party with
more leverage, usually the parent at the commission end, can still set most
of the terms. "Optimized toward both parties" needs a bargaining rule to mean
something definite. Possible rules range from a bargaining solution such as
Nash's, to the outcome of negotiation under each side's outside option, to
terms the parent offers and the other party accepts or refuses. That range is
established, so the choice belongs to charter design.

The five statuses also raise structural questions that the framework has to
settle:

- **Who assigns a status.** Member and associate are mutual, because both
  sides agree to the articles or the contract. Neutral, opponent, and enemy
  could be self-declared or assigned by the charter. If the charter assigns
  them, enemy designation becomes a vanguard tool, because it puts a group
  into the charter's objectives.
- **Whether individuals hold associate status.** The author's contract answer
  describes a relation between a parent and a subsidiary charter. The
  material question was first about individuals. If individuals can be
  associates too, the same contract model covers both.
- **How the statuses relate to the contract period.** A charter in its
  contract period appears to be an associate of its parent. If so, associate
  is the status that every initiation passes through, and enablement is the
  move from associate to member.

### Left to charter design

The following choices have an established range of options, so each
charter's design makes them:

- The bargaining rule that sets the contract's terms
- What happens when a contract fails, such as reverting to independence,
  dissolving, or extending the contract period
- Whether neutrals inside a jurisdiction count as consenting by default
  (opt-out) or as staying out (opt-in)
- How a charter treats each non-member status, consistent with the earlier
  position that non-members get whatever the articles prescribe
- How a charter moves a party between statuses, such as from enemy to
  opponent
- How a charter weighs participation among members, such as quorums or
  weighting by intensity

## Effect on the glossary

This section reflects all four rounds of answers. Integrating them changes
the following entries:

- **Charter formation.** Replace the list of three routes with three facts: a
  charter can exist ex natura, a party joins a parent along the gradient
  between voluntary admission and interventionist commission, and a parent
  forms only by converting a contract among several parties.
- **Admission and act of commission.** Describe each as one end of the
  gradient. Both pass through associate status under a contract and end in
  enablement.
- **Independent formation.** Merge into the ex natura case, or rename to
  match it.
- **Voluntary association.** The author's term "voluntary admission" is close
  to this existing term but names a different end. One of the two needs a
  rename or a merge.
- **Parent corporation.** Add that a parent forms by converting a contract,
  and that each party has to ratify the conversion.
- **New entries.** Add the contract, conversion, enablement, associate,
  neutral, opponent, and enemy. The enemy entry carries the amendment-range
  test. Opponent and enemy belong near the limit of arbitration.
- **Member, personal corporation, and jurisdiction.** Link to the new
  statuses. Note that statuses differ by commons, that individuals associate
  as personal corporations, and that any human organization can be a party
  to a contract. The jurisdiction entry's rule for non-members then applies
  to each status separately.
- **Highest human charter.** Revise the diagram so that it doesn't imply a
  single top. Many charters can sit directly under the Natural Charter.

## Second round

The first round ended with five decisions. The author answered four of them
on October 27, 2026, and asked for more detail on the fifth.

### Author's position

The author gave the following answers:

1. **The charter assigns every non-member status.** A party is an enemy when
   no amendment that the charter's mechanism allows would permit an
   arbitrated reconciliation that moves the party to neutral or associate
   status, short of dissolving the charter. The author's example is slave
   owners under the jurisdiction of an emancipation charter.
1. **Individuals associate as personal corporations.** The contract model
   applies to people through the existing personal corporation entry.
1. **The contract period is associate status.** Enablement is the move from
   associate to member.
1. **The contract sets exit terms jointly.** This answers decision 3 of the
   undemocratic higher charter review.

### Reassessment

The enemy test is the most consequential answer, because it gives the status
an operational definition:

- **Enemy status depends on position, not identity.** The test compares a
  party's current position with the range that the charter's amendment
  mechanism can reach. A slave owner who frees their slaves leaves that
  position and can become a neutral. The test doesn't name groups.
- **Entrenchment creates enemies.** A charter whose core articles can't be
  amended, or need a very high amendment threshold, has a narrow reachable
  range, so it has more enemies. A charter with broad amendment powers has
  fewer. The number of enemies is a cost of entrenchment that a designer can
  see in advance.
- **Assignment can be checked.** The charter assigns the status, so a
  vanguard could misuse it. But the test has a definite answer: either an
  allowed amendment reaches reconciliation or none does. A fidelity audit or
  an arbitrating parent can review a designation against the charter's own
  amendment rules. This check limits the misuse risk that the first round
  raised.
- **The test reuses the limit of arbitration at charter scale.** The limit of
  arbitration says that some conflicts have no shared interest that can
  settle them. The enemy test locates that limit for one charter, through its
  own amendment space.

The other answers make the model uniform. Every party, whether a person or a
charter, joins the same way: associate status under a joint contract, then
enablement to membership. Because a personal corporation is a corporation,
the five statuses need only one set of rules.

### The contract at the voluntary end

Decision 5 asks what happens at the voluntary end of the gradient when no
parent charter exists. At that end, autonomous struggle establishes a charter
where it stands, and the charter then holds a contract until admission. A
contract needs a second party, and admission needs something to be admitted
into. Three situations leave the charter without one:

- **Inside an existing state.** A tenants' union, a cooperative, or a
  movement forms within a state that isn't a charter. The state already
  claims jurisdiction over the commons. This is the situation that decision 5
  of the undemocratic higher charter review describes.
- **Among peers.** Several independent charters exist, but none is a parent.
  They can form a parent together, as a federation does.
- **With nobody.** The commons has no existing authority, such as a new
  technology, an online space, or the first charter of the movement. The
  highest human charter is always in this situation, because only the
  Natural Charter sits above it.

Each situation has a candidate answer:

| Situation | Candidate answer | Precedent | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inside an existing state | Treat the state as a counterparty. The state's law becomes the contract terms, and the charter is in effect the state's associate. | A cooperative incorporated under state law, a union recognized under labor law | The state sets most terms. Dependency is visible but not reduced. |
| Inside an existing state | Treat the state as a neutral, opponent, or enemy under the enemy test. No contract exists, only struggle. | Dual power. The meditations' revolutionary sanctuary. | The charter has no legal standing, and the state can treat it as a threat. |
| Among peers | The peers' contracts with each other become the parent's founding articles. Enablement is the moment the parent forms. | The US ratification by state conventions | Consistent with the model. Needs no new concept. |
| With nobody | The contract period is empty. The charter is enabled on establishment and relates to everything outside it through the five statuses. | The highest human charter. A first charter in a new domain. | The vanguard that initiates the charter faces no counterparty during the provisional period. |

The two answers for an existing state aren't exclusive. A movement can be a
registered cooperative and an opponent of the same state's housing policy.
So the decision behind decision 5 is structural: can one party hold different
statuses toward one charter for different commons? If it can, the existing
state is an associate on the commons where the charter accepts its law and an
opponent or enemy on the commons where it doesn't. The leverage answer in the
preface suggests that the framework needs this split. A movement uses legal
standing where it's available and struggle where it isn't, and the gradient
of leverage across the globe decides which applies where.

The empty case is the founding paradox with nothing left to bound it. The
first charter of the movement, and the highest human charter, start without
any party that can hold the initiator to a contract. If the framework needs a
check there, the only candidates are the initiator's own members once they
join, through ratification or a sunset on the provisional articles.

## Third round

The author answered the case of a charter with no counterparty on October 27,
2026.

### Author's position

Creating a parent needs another party. Without one, admission means nothing,
because the charter is still the same organization. A charter can exist *ex
natura*, without a parent. A parent forms only when a contract among several
parties is converted into a charter, with its own organization that holds
jurisdiction over all of those parties.

### Reassessment

The answer corrects the second round's "with nobody" case and gives the
framework a single rule for how parents form:

- **A charter ex natura has no contract period.** The second round described
  its contract period as empty. That framing was wrong. Admission and
  enablement apply only when a second party exists, so a charter that forms
  on its own simply exists under the Natural Charter. It relates to every
  other party through the five statuses.
- **Every parent is a converted contract.** At the voluntary end, several
  parties hold contracts with each other, and converting those contracts
  creates the parent. At the commission end, the parent already exists
  because an earlier conversion created it. So the gradient describes how a
  party joins, and conversion describes how a parent comes to exist.
- **Many charters can sit directly under the Natural Charter.** Any charter
  without a parent is the top of its own structure. The highest human charter
  entry describes one top, the largest coalition with a real shared interest.
  Under this answer, it's the largest of many parentless charters, which
  matches the entry's own definition but not its diagram.
- **The founding paradox for a charter ex natura is an ordinary founding
  problem.** Whether its first articles need ratification by its members, a
  sunset, or neither is a choice with an established range of options. Under
  the scope boundary, the choice belongs to charter design.

Conversion raises one new question. The parties to the contract each have
their own amendment procedures. Converting the contract into a parent puts
each party under a higher-order constraint. Decision 2 of the undemocratic
higher charter review asked whether joining a parent is an amendment that
needs the lower charter's ratification. Conversion is the moment that
decision applies to.

## Fourth round

The author answered the three remaining decisions on October 27, 2026.

### Author's position

The author gave the following answers:

1. **One party can hold different statuses toward a charter for different
   commons.** The author's analogy is companies registered under the same
   state that compete against one another.
1. **Any human organization can be a party to a contract.** This includes an
   existing state that isn't a charter, and it extends down to the
   individual. The author's example is a contract with a ceremonial monarch.
1. **Converting a contract into a parent needs each party's ratification.**
   Without ratification, a party would only be a neutral or an associate,
   subject to the parent's influence without being represented inside it.

### Reassessment

The answers close every open decision in this document and make the model
general:

- **A status is a set of relations, not a single label.** A party's status
  toward a charter can differ by commons, so it works like the
  representation vector: one entry for each commons the two parties share.
  The enemy test then applies per commons. A party can be an enemy on one
  commons and an associate on another.
- **The contract model reaches outside the framework.** A party to a contract
  doesn't have to be a charter. So the framework can describe its relations
  with states, firms, offices, and people that don't use it, without
  requiring them to adopt it first. This gives the Social Charter a way to
  operate inside the existing world, which the leverage answer in the
  preface depends on.
- **Ratification protects representation.** The author's reason for answer 3
  sets a rule: no party is bound as a member without being represented
  inside the parent. A party that doesn't ratify stays outside, as a
  neutral, associate, or opponent.

### Effect on the undemocratic higher charter review

The fourth round answers or narrows four of that review's five decisions:

- **Decision 1, a parent without members' authority.** Narrowed. A party that
  didn't ratify the conversion isn't a member of the resulting parent. The
  parent can still influence it, but only as an outside party that holds a
  status toward it. Whether an imposed authority counts as a charter at all
  is still the author's call.
- **Decision 2, joining as an amendment.** Answered. Conversion needs each
  party's ratification.
- **Decision 3, who sets exit terms.** Answered in the second round. The
  contract sets them jointly.
- **Decision 5, relations with an existing non-charter state.** Answered. A
  charter relates to such a state through contracts and statuses, and the
  statuses can differ by commons.

Decision 4, whether democracy is a framework requirement or a design choice,
stays open. Ratification makes consent at the moment of conversion a
framework requirement. It doesn't settle how a parent governs itself after
conversion.
