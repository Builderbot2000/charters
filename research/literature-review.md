# Literature review of the Social Charter glossary

This review compares the Social Charter glossary with existing research. It
has two aims: to avoid rebuilding ideas that already exist, and to avoid
reviving ideas that research has already undermined. It also assesses whether
the overall synthesis is new compared with recent work.

Status as of October 22, 2026.

## Scope and method

The review covers all 14 sections of the glossary in
[`site/content/terms`](../site/content/terms). It draws on political economy,
social choice, mechanism design, federalism, constitutional theory,
principal–agent theory, and AI alignment.

The review has these limits:

- The comparison with older literature comes from general knowledge, not
  from a systematic search.
- The novelty assessment rests on about 10 web searches. A search that finds
  nothing doesn't prove that nothing exists.
- Some details come from secondary summaries. Check each citation against its
  primary source before you publish it.

| Term | Nearest established term | Difference |
|---|---|---|

## Conflicts with established literature

No core idea in the glossary has been refuted. The following claims sit on
the contested or losing side of a real debate, and the glossary doesn't yet
acknowledge it. They're ordered by how much they threaten the core thesis.

1. **Big tent and Olson.** Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965)
   shows that large, diffuse groups organize worse than small, concentrated
   ones. *The Rise and Decline of Nations* (1982) argues that dense networks
   of organized interests entrench the status quo. The big tent is a
   strategy that depends on effective coordination, so the test is whether
   charters lower the cost of organizing enough to meet that condition.
   Riker's size principle (1962) predicts minimal coalitions, but its
   empirical support is weak.
1. **Obscured consensus and transparency in negotiation.** Prat ("The Wrong
   Kind of Transparency," 2005), Stasavage ("Polarization and Publicity,"
   2007), and Elster find that public positions can make concessions harder
   and that closed negotiation can help compromise. Disclosure is well
   supported in markets, but the SEC analogy doesn't carry over to bargaining
   unchanged. This is the most direct challenge to a premise.
1. **Explicit positions and voter belief systems.** Converse (1964) and Achen
   and Bartels (*Democracy for Realists*, 2016) find that most citizens lack
   stable, coherent issue positions. Stated preferences and the specification
   of each meeting of interests assume positions that people can state.
1. **Issue unbundling and preferences that depend on each other.** When a
   preference on one issue depends on another, voting issue by issue can
   produce a combination that almost nobody wants (Brams, Kilgour, and
   Zwicker, 1998; Lacy and Niou, 2000). Unbundling also removes logrolling.
   Shepsle's structure-induced equilibrium (1979) supports unbundling
   instead.
1. **Objective functions and multitask principal–agent theory.** Holmström
   and Milgrom (1991) show that strong incentives on measurable goals damage
   goals that can't be measured. Bevan and Hood (2006) document target gaming
   in the NHS. Hayek's knowledge problem (1945) challenges decentralized
   information combined with centralized execution.
1. **Welfare and interpersonal comparison.** Letting each person's will set
   the weights rules out comparing welfare between people. Arrow's
   impossibility result depends on that lack of comparison, and Sen (1970)
   showed that allowing comparison escapes it. Treating a shaped will as
   reduced freedom also needs an outside standard. "Obligations are costs"
   is disputed by Frankfurt, Raz, and care ethics.
1. **The SEC analogy and capture theory.** Stigler (1971) argues that
   regulators tend to be captured by the industries they regulate.
1. **Coalition-decided exclusion and the boundary problem.** Whelan (1983)
   and later work treat the boundary of a democracy as impossible to decide
   democratically. Goodin's all-affected principle (2007) is the main
   alternative.
1. **Rules as code and the record of DAOs.** The DAO exploit (2016), the
   Beanstalk governance attack (2022), low turnout, and control by large
   token holders are all documented. Kaplow (1992) explains why some
   provisions work better as standards than as rules.
1. **Metastability and the edge of chaos.** Mitchell, Hraber, and
   Crutchfield (1993) failed to replicate the original edge-of-chaos result.
   The ecumenical charter example should use only the weak claim, that too
   much order and too little order are both failure modes.

## Answers from the literature

Some open questions in the glossary have established answers:

- Quadratic voting (Lalley and Weyl; Posner and Weyl, *Radical Markets*,
  2018) is a specified mechanism for letting each person's will set the
  weights.
- Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970) shows that exit can weaken
  voice, because the members most able to leave are often the ones who would
  push for reform.
- Hayek, and Stanley and Lehman, answer the generativity gap by running many
  independent experiments instead of measuring discovery. Sibling
  corporations and exit already provide this.
- Yardstick competition formalizes the fidelity audit's sibling controls.
- Evidence on mandatory auditor rotation is mixed. The EU adopted it in 2014,
  and the US rejected it, partly because rotation loses expertise.
- Lijphart's consociationalism offers tested schemes for weighting groups,
  which power balancing doesn't yet define.

## Novelty compared with recent work

The parts of the Social Charter aren't new. The following work comes closest
to the whole system:

- **Ostrom's Institutional Analysis and Development (IAD) framework and
  McGinnis's networks of adjacent action situations (2011).** These break
  governance into game-like action situations linked in a polycentric
  network. They anticipate the common blueprint, but they're descriptive. A
  2023 systematic review found that methods for putting them into practice
  remain nascent.
- **Modular Politics (Schneider and others, 2021).** This work turns IAD into
  composable governance modules for online communities.
- **Grassroots Federation (arXiv 2505.02208, 2025) and Federated Assemblies
  (AAAI, 2025).** These model a graph of communities that join parent
  federations by mutual consent and can leave on their own. They focus on
  fair representation through sortition, not on functional interest charters.
- **Weyl and Tang, *Plurality* (2024).** This book shares the view that
  identity comes from overlapping groups. It has less constitutional
  architecture.
- **Koster and others, "Human-centred mechanism design with Democratic AI"
  (2022).** In this study, people vote on the objective and an AI designs the
  mechanism.
- **Futarchy in practice.** Optimism's 2025 trial found that forecasts
  overestimated outcomes and that participants had an incentive to game the
  measured results.

The following contributions don't appear in the work found:

1. One recursive charter type that spans every level, from the personal
   corporation to the highest human charter
1. Two modes of resolution, arbitration under a shared parent or bounded
   struggle, with the empty-core test deciding which applies and a coalition
   deciding the top
1. A constitutional design that meets the coordination condition that the
   big-tent strategy depends on
1. A fidelity audit for each charter, tied to a capability measure of welfare

A defensible position is to present the Social Charter as a prescriptive
development of Ostrom's polycentric framework that uses mechanism design.
Before claiming novelty in public, read Grassroots Federation, McGinnis
(2011), and Modular Politics in full.

## Open decisions

These decisions and tasks are still open, as of October 28, 2026:

- Decide whether to add new intellectual relatives: IAD and McGinnis,
  Grassroots Federation, *Plurality*, futarchy, FOCJ, Michels, Hirschman, and
  Nozick.
- Decide whether to add two open problems: the Olson problem and multitask
  distortion.
- Soften the metastability language in the ecumenical charter entry.
- Mention the Open problems section in the page introduction in
  [`site.yaml`](../site/content/site.yaml).

Later reviews settled the following items:

- **Contested premises.** Obscured consensus is narrowed to transparency of
  the problem. Issue bundling's separability problem is folded into the
  decomposability premise. How big tents pool resources is left to charter
  design. See the [premise review](intent-and-premises.md).
- **Boundary problem and proxy will.** Exclusion is now status assignment,
  which each charter makes, and a proxy for people who can't will is a
  choice for each charter.
- **Stated preferences.** Whether they move the working objective is left
  to charter design.
- **Neutrality of capability welfare.** Moot. Welfare is no longer the
  framework's measure, only one that a charter can adopt.
- **Commons.** Redefined on October 28, 2026, to cover intangible holdings,
  such as knowledge, cultural heritage, reputation, and contracts with
  associates. It follows the knowledge commons work of Hess and Ostrom and
  Tirole's collective reputation, and it's a common property regime, not a
  common-pool resource.
- **Intervention.** Renamed *recourse* on October 28, 2026, because
  *intervention* means interference in a sovereign state in international
  relations. *Contingency* names the event that triggers a recourse.
- **Generativity gap.** Rewritten on October 28, 2026. How much generativity
  a charter needs, and how it pursues it, is a choice for its design. A
  charter optimizes generativity analytically, through sibling experiments
  and a decentralized, delegated structure, not quantitatively, because no
  measure captures it accurately. The entry credits Hayek and Stanley and
  Lehman.
