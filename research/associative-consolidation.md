# Associative consolidation

This document looks at how a party moves between base associate and full
member of a charter, and what the framework says about that movement. The
author calls this movement *associative consolidation*. It applies to people
and to organizations alike, because an individual takes part as a personal
corporation.

The topic came out of a broader discussion that the author raised. That
discussion covered the following subjects:

- The framework's fit with internet-based communication
- The gig-style evolution of organized action, in commercial platforms and
  in Ukraine's armed forces
- The merit of ephemeral single-interest membership over persistent political
  identity

The author named consolidation as the most important question in that
discussion. Persistent membership usually produces the benefits of
specialization, in the sense of Adam Smith's division of labor. The question
is how a charter can keep those benefits when membership is no longer
persistent by default.

Status as of October 28, 2026. The author answered three rounds of decisions
on the same day, and this document records those answers. The answers are
integrated into the glossary, as
[Effect on the glossary](#effect-on-the-glossary) describes.

## Background

The glossary already holds the pieces that gig-style membership needs. A gig
is a contract between a personal corporation and a charter, held in associate
status, with no intent to reach admission. The associate entry already allows
a party to stay an associate indefinitely.

### Gig-style organized action

Two kinds of cases show the pattern:

- **Ukraine's armed forces.** GIS Arta matches targets to whichever firing
  unit is free. Delta pools situational awareness from many sources. The Army
  of Drones bonus pays points for verified strikes, and units spend the
  points on the Brave1 Market. The IT Army of Ukraine hands out tasks to
  volunteers over Telegram. In each case, a persistent hierarchy sets the
  objective and a fluid crowd carries it out.
- **Commercial platforms.** Ride-hailing and microtask platforms match
  workers to tasks in the same way. The platform owns the commons that
  matters: the network, the ratings, and the dispatch data. Workers stay
  associates permanently and have no representation.

The Ukrainian cases fit delegation of functions closely, and they support the
pluralist vanguard thesis. They work because the objective is uncontested,
though. Gig coordination solves execution. It doesn't answer the Question of
Arbitration. The commercial cases show the failure mode: a parent that never
went through conversion, with associates who are influenced without being
represented. This matches the platform-owner route in the undemocratic higher
charter review.

### Ephemeral membership and persistent identity

Persistent political identity bundles issues at the level of the person, in
the same way that elections bundle them at the level of the vote (Mason,
*Uncivil Agreement*, 2018). Ephemeral single-interest membership unbundles
both. The glossary already makes enemy status depend on position, not
identity.

The literature on digital movements gives the counterargument. Low-cost
mobilization skips the slow organizing that builds decision-making capacity,
so large movements can't negotiate or change tactics (Tufekci, *Twitter and
Tear Gas*, 2017). Bennett and Segerberg describe the same pattern as
*connective action* ("The Logic of Connective Action," 2012). Ephemeral
membership also weakens the big tent, because members who don't stay don't
trade support across issues. That makes assumption A1 in the premise review
harder to defend.

### Specialization needs specific investment

Smith observed that the division of labor is limited by the extent of the
market (*The Wealth of Nations*, 1776, book 1, chapter 3). Gig networks
enlarge the market, so they encourage specialization in skills that are
useful anywhere. Becker separates those *general* skills from *specific*
skills, which are valuable only inside one organization (*Human Capital*,
1964).

Specific investment is what persistent membership protects. A party that
commits a specific investment can be held up: once it's committed, the other
side can renegotiate against it (Klein, Crawford, and Alchian, 1978.
Williamson, *The Economic Institutions of Capitalism*, 1985). Williamson
places hybrid forms between market and hierarchy, which is the organizational
version of a consolidation gradient. Hansmann argues that ownership goes to
the class of patrons whose costs of contracting through the market are
highest (*The Ownership of Enterprise*, 1996).

So the open market already handles general specialization. A charter needs
consolidation only to protect specific investment without requiring
permanent membership.

## Author's position

The author answered in two rounds.

### First round

1. **Tiers are discrete.** Charter articles are discrete, language-based
   provisions, so the terms of membership are discrete too. Charters defined
   semantically are possible in principle, but they would stay toy examples.
1. **Dimensions and rules belong to charter design.** Which dimensions a tier
   varies, and what rule links them, are choices for each charter. The
   framework doesn't set them.
1. **Consolidation runs both ways.** A party can move toward full membership
   or back toward associate status. The author also intends to study how a
   charter scales its centralization over time. Some periods favor
   consolidating membership and jurisdiction, and others favor dispersing
   them. Deng Xiaoping's reforms are an example.
1. **There's no ceiling.** The framework doesn't limit how far a personal
   corporation can consolidate. A limit would be arbitrary and would break
   the framework's neutrality.

### Second round

1. **Fundamental protections belong to charter design.** The framework stops
   requiring them. Instead, the framework defines modes of intervention
   against a malign charter, such as unilateral exit (secession) and
   arbitration from the level above.
1. **Centralization is a parameter of the charter.** A charter's articles
   control its degree of centralization, and the charter optimizes it
   according to its own design.
1. **The framework's job is a standardized model.** At framework scope, the
   glossary clearly delineates the parameters, states, and outputs of a
   charter and of its dimensions, such as jurisdiction, membership, and
   commons. A standardized model makes each charter a known problem, so that
   heuristics from well-studied theories apply to it.

### Third round

1. **Intervention modes.** The author confirmed the list of modes and the
   split between arbitrated and noncooperative intervention.
1. **Fundamental protections leave the glossary.** They become an aspect of
   charter design. What works depends too much on the scenario, such as a war,
   an economic crisis, or social unrest, for the framework to prescribe
   anything general.
1. **The model skeleton.** The author adopted the proposed skeleton for now
   and expects to expand it greatly.

## Reassessment

The answers narrow the framework. They also give it a clearer task.

### What the answers settle

- **Admission becomes one transition among many.** The glossary treats
  admission as the single step from associate to member. Under answers 1 and
  3, a charter can define any number of discrete tiers between the two, and a
  party can move in either direction.
- **The example designs leave the framework.** The previous discussion
  proposed dimensions such as subjection, specific investment, voice,
  protection, exit cost, and horizon, and a rule that voice tracks specific
  exposure. These designs stay useful as examples for charter designers, but
  they aren't framework claims.
- **The framework sets no floor.** Answer 4 and the move of fundamental
  protections to charter design follow the earlier decision that the
  framework fixes no minimum of capabilities beneath members' will.

### Consolidation and centralization are one mechanism

Consolidation describes a party's movement. Centralization describes the
charter's overall shape. They're the same mechanism seen from two sides:

- A charter centralizes by consolidating parties toward full membership,
  absorbing subsidiaries, and holding jurisdiction at its own level.
- A charter disperses by moving parties back to associate tiers and handing
  jurisdiction out by commission.

Under the second-round answers, both are parameters that the charter's
articles control. The framework only has to name them as parameters, so that
a charter's design can set them and theories of centralization can apply.
Oates's decentralization theorem and the literature on fiscal federalism
are the obvious candidates (Oates, *Fiscal Federalism*, 1972). Qian and
Weingast's account of "market-preserving federalism" analyzes the Deng
reforms in these terms (1997).

One problem stays open for the author's planned study. Consolidating is
easier than dispersing again. The Roman dictatorship was centralization with
a term set in advance, and it failed once the term stopped binding. What makes
recentralization reversible is likely a design question, like the capture
question that the author routed to constitutional design on October 24,
2026.

### Intervention happens whether or not the articles allow it

The glossary already names two conflict modes. A higher charter arbitrates a
conflict, or the parties settle it where it is by noncooperative struggle.
Intervention against a malign charter follows the same split. Parties can
always act against a charter by struggle. A charter's articles only decide
which interventions are arbitrated, and on what terms.

This split lets the framework define the modes without requiring any of
them. The author confirmed the following modes in the third round:

| Mode | Who acts | Arbitrated form | Existing term |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exit | A member | Withdrawal under the exit procedure | Exit |
| Secession | A subsidiary or a faction | Withdrawal with a share of the commons, on terms set in advance | None |
| Escalation | A member or subsidiary | Arbitration by the level above | Escalation, arbitration |
| Audit | The parent or a commission | Review of execution against the articles | Audit, fidelity audit |
| Demotion | The parent | Moving the charter back from member to associate | None, close to status |
| Receivership | The parent | Temporary control of the charter's executive functions | None |
| Dissolution | The members or the parent | Ending the charter under its dissolution clause | Dissolution |
| Reclassification | A peer or the parent | Treating the charter as an opponent or an enemy | Status, opponent, enemy |

Without arbitration, each mode becomes struggle. Exit becomes desertion,
secession becomes a war of independence, and demotion becomes sanctions.

### A standardized model of the charter

Answer 3 of the second round restates the core thesis: each meeting of
interests becomes a constrained, known problem. A standardized model makes
that concrete. The following skeleton is a proposal that collects terms the
glossary already defines:

- **Dimensions.** Commons, jurisdiction, membership, articles, and delegated
  functions
- **Parameters.** Settings that the articles control, such as the tiers of
  membership, the degree of centralization, amendment thresholds, and exit
  terms
- **States.** The charter's life-cycle stage, and each party's status and
  tier on each commons
- **Transitions.** Admission, consolidation, deconsolidation, commission,
  conversion, exit, secession, escalation, and dissolution
- **Outputs.** Decisions, executions, and measurements

Each part maps to theories that already exist. Mechanism design applies to
decisions, principal-agent theory to delegated functions, transaction cost
economics to consolidation, fiscal federalism to centralization, and Ostrom's
work on the commons to jurisdiction.

## Effect on the glossary

The following changes reflect all three rounds of answers:

- **Removed.** Fundamental protections, tiered constitutional design,
  meta-governance, substantive constitution, operations, anti-capture
  protection, and charter lifecycle. The charter lifecycle entry stated the
  principle behind the tiers, so it went with them, along with its section.
- **Recast.** Amendment, amendment threshold, and living charter now refer to
  the charter's own articles instead of constitutional layers. Exit is a mode
  of intervention that the framework doesn't guarantee. Interpreter capture
  stays an open problem, generalized from protections to any article written
  in language.
- **Updated.** Admission, status, and associate describe tiers and
  consolidation in both directions. The associate entry names the gig as
  the simplest case.
- **Added.** Associative consolidation, centralization, intervention,
  secession, demotion, receivership, and charter model. The evolution and
  protection section is renamed "Evolution and intervention."
