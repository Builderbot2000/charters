# Creed and decomposability

This record covers a working session on November 15, 2026. The session read
[meditation 7](../references/meditation-7%202026-11-14.txt), "The Court Eunuch
and the Interregnum," against the Framework. It ended with the finding that
the meditation's creed problem falls under decisions the author had already
made about decomposability, charter design, and endogenous preferences.

## Decisions

The author settled the following points:

- **The creed question is the one to explore.** The question is whether a
  society like the present United States still holds the soil for a united
  creed. The alternative is a gradual devolution from a unified nation-state
  with a common civic religion to a localized, feudal structure built on
  peer-to-peer affiliations and federated hierarchies.
- **A social contract is a creed, and a creed buys loyalty.** Meditation 7
  argues that contract can't buy the loyalty needed to rule. That argument
  covers financial contracts only. A charter is a social contract, so the
  argument doesn't apply to it.
- **Fully automated administration is unlikely for decades.** Authority
  resists its own displacement in a way that labor can't. Someone above labor
  decides to automate it, but officials decide whether to automate themselves.
  The session drops the creedless, automated state as a likely outcome.
- **A creed is thick before victory and thin only after it.** The early church
  was demanding, not thin. A thin creed that sits over many fragments appears
  only once a movement has won.
- **The answer already exists in the Framework.** Decomposability, the
  degrees of freedom in charter design, and the resolution of endogenous
  preferences together cover the creed problem. See
  [How the Framework answers](#how-the-framework-answers).

## Meditation 7 and the premises

Meditation 7 concludes that the American interregnum is most likely resolved,
if at all, by a decentralized networked movement with a universal creed. Its
open question is whether such a movement can build state-level
infrastructure, beat a rival coalition funded by corporate courtiers, and stay
distributed after it wins. That question maps onto the two premises:

- Failing to form and enact an agenda is the
  [question of arbitration](../site/content/terms/question-of-arbitration.mdoc).
- Failing to stay distributed after victory is the
  [vanguard interest](../site/content/terms/vanguard-interest.mdoc).

The meditation rules out a vanguard because of the American federalist
tradition, so the question of arbitration is the binding premise in this
scenario. Other links to the glossary include the following:

- *Courtier power* is influence through access, gatekeeping, and hedging
  across successors. It's a case of
  [obscured consensus](../site/content/terms/obscured-consensus.mdoc).
- *The inertial state* governs on momentum and captured interest. Both texts
  cite Mancur Olson's *The Rise and Decline of Nations* (1982), and the Olson
  problem in [open problems](open-problems.md) names the same mechanism.
- *Gerontocracy* reads as factions of interest that ossify a group, and the
  competition among left-bloc constituencies reads as factions of principle
  that subvert one. See
  [factional interest](../site/content/terms/factional-interest.mdoc).
- *Atrophied blocs* are big tents that pay the full cost of agreeing on a
  whole platform. Meditation 7 finds that material grievances differ by
  geography, so no single class position crosses the urban–rural line. That's
  an empty core in the sense of the
  [big tent](../site/content/terms/big-tent.mdoc) entry.
- *States as sanctuaries* apply meditation 1's uneven spread of technology at
  the domestic scale, which matches the author's answer to the leverage
  problem.
- *Federal–state conflict over force*, fought in court instead of by
  secession, is a conflict that no higher charter settles. It falls to
  [natural resistance](../site/content/terms/natural-resistance.mdoc).

## Soil for a creed

The session found little soil for a substantive, territorial, national creed
of the old kind. The carriers of the last civic creed have weakened or
reversed:

- Print and broadcast media built national publics (Benedict Anderson,
  *Imagined Communities*, 1983). Networked feeds now build communities that
  aren't territorial.
- Mass institutions spread the civic cult, including conscription, the
  mainline Protestant churches, industrial unions, national networks, and a
  common school curriculum.
- No class position crosses the urban–rural line, as meditation 7 argues.
- No ideological rival disciplines the country, as meditation 7 argues about
  China.
- People increasingly live among those who share their views (Bill Bishop,
  *The Big Sort*, 2008).

Full feudal devolution isn't inevitable either. Feudalism followed the
collapse of central fiscal and coercive capacity. The federal state still
collects the taxes, pays the old-age transfers that tie the voting cohort to
the center, and commands the military. Devolution is likelier in allegiance,
culture, and services than in tax and force. Two historical forms fit that
middle state:

- *Pillarization*: the Netherlands from about 1900 to 1960 kept separate
  Catholic, Protestant, socialist, and liberal pillars, each with its own
  schools, press, unions, broadcasters, and party. Elite bargaining held them
  together (Arend Lijphart's *consociationalism*).
- *New medievalism*: Hedley Bull's term for overlapping, non-exclusive
  authorities in place of sovereign territories (*The Anarchical Society*,
  1977).

Fragmentation and a universal creed aren't opposites. Medieval Christendom was
both feudal and united by one church. The creed didn't unify the realms. It
sat above them as a shared layer of legitimacy and arbitration.

## Thick before victory

The early church grew because it was strict. It refused the civic sacrifice,
held a demanding moral code, kept a catechumenate that lasted years, and
excluded every other cult. Rodney Stark argues that this strictness caused its
growth (*The Rise of Christianity*, 1996). Laurence Iannaccone gives the
mechanism: costly commitment screens out free riders, so members gain more
from the group ("Why Strict Churches Are Strong," 1994). The church thinned
only after Constantine and Theodosius, when mass conversion became the price
of establishment.

Fragmentation is good soil for a thick creed. Stark and Roger Finke find that
strict sects grow fastest in plural, unregulated religious markets. The early
church grew among the uprooted in the empire's cities, and Dutch pillarization
began with thick movements. The modern parallel in meditation 7 is the
displaced, credentialed, urban young.

Two dimensions separate the cases, admission and commitment:

| | Thick commitment | Thin commitment |
|---|---|---|
| Open admission | The contender, such as the early church | Big-tent pantheons |
| Closed admission | Identitarian movements | Courtier networks and clubs |

## How the Framework answers

The session first asked whether the Social Charter organizes thin tents, thick
sects, or both. A big tent as the glossary defines it is open and thin, which
is the pantheon form that meditation 7 says can't resolve an interregnum. The
author pointed out that the question was already answered.

### A degree of freedom in charter design

Thick or thin commitment and open or closed admission form a delineated range
of options. Under the framework's scope boundary, such a range is left to
charter design. The Framework is an operator, and a creed's content and
thickness belong to the objective and design that a Question instance
supplies. See the [Framework as an operator](framework-as-operator.md).

### A position in the charter graph

Under [decomposability](optimal-substructure.md), parent and child are the
same kind of entity, so thick and thin charters can nest and still compose.
Each charter is optimal for its own objective, and the parent internalizes the
spillovers among its constituents:

- Pillarization is thick pillars under a thin, consociational parent.
- Christendom after victory kept thick sub-charters in the monastic orders and
  the clergy, and a thin establishment over the realms.

The sequence from thick to thin is the same charter moving up a level. The
[factional interest](../site/content/terms/factional-interest.mdoc) entry
already describes victory: a faction gains enough influence over the commons
to become the higher charter. At that level, its objective becomes the
higher-order common interest of diverse constituents, so it thins there and
stays thick inside its own sub-charter. Before victory, an exclusive sect that
refuses its place among the other cults is in conflict under the
[limit of arbitration](../site/content/terms/limit-of-arbitration.mdoc).

### Deliberate conditioning of preferences

A thick creed shapes its members' wills on purpose, through initiation,
costly signals, and commitment. The author closed endogenous preferences on
November 13, 2026: the objective is a fixed specification that reads wills, so
shaping those wills is ordinary optimization. Three consequences follow:

- A sect that forms loyalty by conditioning its members is a legitimate
  charter design, not a failure of the operator. The initiation gradient
  already gives the Framework a graded, costly entry like the catechumenate.
  See [initiation and admission](initiation-and-admission.md).
- A pantheon is weak because it aggregates wills and shapes none. A charter
  that may shape wills can be a creed instead of a market, which is why a
  social contract buys loyalty when a financial contract doesn't.
- Outside conditioning, such as networked media and displacement by
  artificial intelligence (AI), is a given condition under a higher
  jurisdiction. It forms the soil, not part of the operator.

### What falls where

The creed question splits across the parts of the book:

- **Model:** whether a thick, open sect is forming among the dislocated
- **Question:** which creed, meaning which objective and how thick
- **Framework:** only the need to allow thick and thin charters and to
  compose them, which decomposability already provides

Meditation 7 doesn't expose a gap in the Framework. It's a scenario that tests
the Framework, and the structure holds.

## The automated state

Meditation 7 notes that a corporation can't rule because ruling takes loyal
people, and loyalty takes a creed. If AI ran administration and enforcement,
that check would weaken, and an order could hold without believers. The
session's earlier reply named this the *hollow strong state*.

The author ruled it unlikely for decades, because authority resists its own
displacement. Public choice theory models the same resistance in William
Niskanen's budget-maximizing bureaucrat (1971). One consequence for meditation
7 follows. AI displacement lands on private service labor while the state
keeps its human staff. The state still needs loyalty, so it still needs a
creed. The divide between insulated officials and displaced young outsiders
also widens, which strengthens the meditation's argument about gerontocracy.

## What stays open

The session left these questions open:

- The Model question: whether a thick, open creed is forming in the present
  United States, and among whom
- Goodhart's law as it applies to a thick creed: measures of devotion can
  drift from what the creed is for, as costly signals turn into performance.
  See [open problems](open-problems.md).
- Meditations 2 and 5, which meditation 7 cites but which aren't in
  `references/`
