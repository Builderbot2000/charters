# United Commons narrative

This record covers a working session on November 21, 2026. The session started
from a common claim: successful ideologies and religions tell a three-part
story of a beautiful past, a dark present, and a glorious future. It asked how
socialism fits that pattern, then applied the pattern to United Commons, the
candidate name for a movement that applies the Social Charter. United Commons
names the movement only, not the framework.

## Decisions

The author settled the following points:

- **United Commons has a past by definition.** A commons must already exist
  before anyone can defend it. So every dead or dying convention, practice, or
  context that people held dear can serve as a "good old days" story. Each
  community brings its own lost commons, and the movement doesn't need one
  shared myth.
- **Dead commons are a lead, not a promise.** A commons that can't come back
  still draws people in. The movement uses it as an attraction and doesn't
  promise to restore it.
- **No arrangement is free of cost.** Every arrangement is one group's
  privilege at another group's cost. A fair childhood costs parents time and
  energy, and peace for some people restricts others. No purity test can sort
  real commons from privileges, so the conflict goes to arbitration, which is
  what the project exists for.
- **A commons is a natural right to develop toward.** The movement can defend
  commons as something people ought to be entitled to, even where a given
  commons never existed. The past becomes evidence that commons are
  achievable, and the right is the ground.
- **The story's job is alarm.** The narrative stresses the dark present: act
  now, before further encroachment. Mourning a lost past is secondary.

## Socialism's beautiful past

Socialism fits the three-part pattern, but its past is lost innocence rather
than a glorious era. The session found several versions:

- **Primitive communism.** In *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and
  the State* (1884), Friedrich Engels drew on Lewis Henry Morgan to describe
  early societies with common land, no classes, and no state. The fall is the
  rise of private property. The future is common ownership again "at a higher
  level," so the story is a spiral, not a circle.
- **The early church.** Acts 2:44 says the believers "had all things in
  common." The verse anchored Christian socialism and the Diggers.
- **Natural equality.** John Ball's slogan from the Peasants' Revolt of 1381,
  "When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?", appeals to a
  time before lords.
- **The Norman Yoke.** English radicals imagined free Anglo-Saxons who lost
  their liberties to the Norman conquest.
- **The medieval craftsman.** William Morris, John Ruskin, and the guild
  socialists idealized whole, unalienated work before the factory.
- **The peasant commune.** Russian populists saw the village *mir* as
  socialism already in existence, and Karl Marx took the idea seriously in his
  letters to Vera Zasulich.
- **The precolonial village.** Julius Nyerere's *ujamaa* in Tanzania framed
  African socialism as a return to traditional familyhood.
- **The movement's own past.** Later socialists also drew on their own heroic
  struggles, such as the Paris Commune, Haymarket, and the October Revolution.

The three-part pattern isn't universal. Progress narratives, such as Whig
history, run from a dark past to a better future. The pattern holds more
broadly in a looser form: a goodness, often a latent human nature, that the
present suppresses.

## Edges and answers

The session raised four edges in the "good old days" narrative. The author
answered each one:

| Edge | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| A dead commons can only be restored, and promised restoration sets up disappointment. | Use dead commons as the lead or attraction, not as a promise. |
| Some cherished conventions were one group's privilege at others' cost, not commons. | Every arrangement is a privilege at someone's cost. Arbitration, not a purity test, handles the conflict. |
| "Defend what exists" is conservative in form and might read as a conservative movement. | Defend commons as a natural right to develop toward, even where a commons never existed. |
| Nostalgia trades on grief for what can't return. | The narrative is alarm, not grief: act before further encroachment. |

## Movement and Framework

Natural-right language is a substantive moral claim. United Commons can hold
it as a creed, but the Framework stays neutral about it, because fundamental
protections belong to charter design. See
[Associative consolidation](associative-consolidation.md). A party can
campaign on its values while defending neutral electoral rules in the same
way.

The narrative also fits the finding in
[Creed and decomposability](creed-and-decomposability.md) that a creed is
thick before victory. Each community's lost commons gives the creed local
thickness, and the shared story of defending commons gives it a thin common
layer.

## What stays open

The following points stay open:

- **Credibility of alarm.** Alarm about specific, checkable enclosures, such
  as a sold-off park, a closed forum, or a privatized standard, holds up.
  General alarm that everything people love is disappearing wears out
  quickly. Naming who gains and who pays in each enclosure might keep the
  alarm concrete.
- **Real commons as evidence.** Elinor Ostrom's fieldwork on long-lived
  commons gives a factual past that the movement can cite. The session didn't
  choose which cases to use.
