# Preface

The Social Charter began with a narrower and darker question than the one it
now tries to answer: is tyranny becoming impossible to remove?

## The first question

A regime has always rested on the people who keep it running, such as
soldiers, clerks, police, and workers. Gene Sharp called them the regime's
pillars of support. When enough of them refused to cooperate, the regime fell,
and Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan found that mass noncooperation succeeded
about twice as often as armed revolt. Information technology and automation
shrink the number of people a state needs, so each refusal counts for less. A
population that confronts an advanced state directly can no longer win.

Following that worry through made it sharper. Ordinary people are losing
leverage on several fronts at once:

- Economic: automation moves advanced economies toward the model of an oil
  state. Rentier-state theory, developed by Hossein Mahdavy and Michael Ross,
  shows that rulers who don't need their citizens' taxes and labor don't need
  their consent either. Luke Drago and Rudolf Laine call the AI version the
  *intelligence curse*.
- Capability: demand for human skill narrows to a small, highly selected
  group, a pattern that David Autor describes as the hollowing out of
  middle-skill work.
- Exit: the world is closed. Albert Hirschman showed that the option to leave
  is what gives voice its force, and James Scott traced how states once
  stopped at the hills and frontiers where people fled them. Technology has
  removed that friction of distance, and no frontier is left to leave for.
- Competition: Douglass North, Barry Weingast, Daron Acemoglu, and James
  Robinson showed that inclusive states used to win because people were the
  productive base. Automation reverses that advantage and rewards extraction.
  With no arbiter above the competition, values that cost an advantage get
  competed away, the dynamic Scott Alexander named *Moloch*.
- Elite conflict: when a ruling group splits, power moves among its factions,
  not down to the population. Selectorate theory, from Bruce Bueno de Mesquita
  and colleagues, predicts that a ruler who depends on a small coalition pays
  that coalition and neglects everyone else.

At the end of this line of reasoning, change no longer rests on the strength
of ordinary people. It rests on the conscience of a few, and resistance
becomes a request for charity.

## The turn

The same forces that erode leverage also keep the order unstable. Technology
spreads faster than anyone can control it, because copying costs far less than
inventing. Places develop unevenly, which leaves gaps where new capabilities
take hold before incumbents can absorb them. Incumbents carry legacy systems
that newcomers don't, the advantage of backwardness that Alexander
Gerschenkron described. Langdon Winner and George Orwell both argued that
technologies favor some holders of power over others. A new technology is more
likely to suit the broad, flexible many than the narrow needs of an
established institution.

So tyranny can't be removed by the means of the 20th century, but the dynamics
of the 21st century can still remove it.

## The problem that remained

That conclusion answers an easier question than the one it started from. It
shows that the order can be displaced, not that ordinary people gain power
when it is. History is discouraging on this point. Openings go to whoever is
best organized when the turbulence settles. In 1917 the workers' councils
broke the old order, and the Bolsheviks held what came after. Zeynep Tufekci
observed the same weakness in movements organized through social media: they
mobilize huge crowds quickly, but without the organization to turn pressure
into lasting gains.

Two failures stand in the way, and they became the framework's premises:

- The *question of arbitration*: decentralized mass movements generate
  momentum but can't form and enact a clear agenda.
- The *vanguard interest*: centralized movements can act, but their leadership
  develops interests of its own and pulls the movement away from the people it
  serves.

The dilemma is old. In *What Is to Be Done?* (1902), Lenin argued for a
disciplined cadre of professional revolutionaries. Rosa Luxemburg answered
that such centralism would stifle the movement's own initiative and put the
leadership in its place.

A second line of thought, about long periods of peace, arrived at the same
place. Long peace compounds early advantage and shields it from the disorder
that would reset it, so orders ossify. Walter Scheidel found that inequality
has fallen sharply only through violent shocks, and Thomas Piketty showed how
returns on capital compound in stable periods. Inequality grows as ordinary
people lose the capacity to make peace costly, which matches Acemoglu and
Robinson's finding that elites concede only when repression costs more than
concession. Technology is the force most likely to crack a sealed order, and
decentralization is what keeps the order open to challenge afterward.

## What ordinary people can still change

Of the strengths that ordinary people are losing, organizing in one place
can't restore one of them: being needed as labor. It can restore another:
acting together.

Developed society is divided in two directions. Single-interest movements cut
vertically across classes, and class divides cut horizontally through those
movements. Class politics in the socialist tradition assumed a homogeneous
class that no longer exists. Single-issue movements gather many people but
rarely achieve more than cosmetic change. Each division fragments the other,
and divided interests leave the status quo in place.

## The answer proposed

The Social Charter is a candidate substrate for coordinating across both
divisions at once. It's meant as a *pluralist vanguard*: it keeps the
coordination and direction of a traditional vanguard, and it gains the
spontaneous momentum and innovation of horizontal movements. A vanguard used
to be the only affordable way to give a movement direction, because
coordinating many voices precisely cost too much. Information technology now
makes that coordination possible.

The framework makes overlapping interests explicit. Each *meeting of
interests* becomes a defined problem, and members install a mechanism designed
for that problem instead of relying on private deals or a leader's discretion.
Broad coalitions, or *big tents*, can then form around what their members
share without first agreeing on everything else. Every group produces
factions, and the framework mediates them toward results that benefit
everyone, instead of leaving them to drift toward entrenchment or subversion.

## Leverage across borders

Coordination alone doesn't answer the loss of leverage. Leverage can be scarce
in one place, but it's unlikely to be scarce everywhere at once.

Technology spreads unevenly, and the gaps between places are widening.
Progress at the frontier is accelerating, while the pace at which technology
spreads stays roughly constant. New inventions are spontaneous in form and in
where they take effect, so regulation can't become uniform across the world
quickly. The unevenness that weakens ordinary people in one country leaves
openings in another.

Under strong rule, revolutionary pressure has usually needed a base outside
the regime's reach. Idean Salehyan found that rebels with sanctuary across a
border last far longer than rebels without it. Lenin organized from
Switzerland, and the African National Congress organized from Zambia and
Tanzania. Leverage that's scarce at home can also come from abroad. Margaret
Keck and Kathryn Sikkink called this the boomerang pattern: when activists'
own government blocks them, allies in other countries press it from outside,
as the boycotts and divestment campaigns against apartheid did.

Ordinary people are the least able to move to where leverage is, so a movement
has to reach both places. That's why the Social Charter is meant for movements
that work across borders, not inside one country.

Support from abroad usually comes with a patron, and a patron has interests of
its own. The Social Charter separates a charter's principles from their
execution. A charter's principles are public, and its strategy stays inside
its executive functions, which can remain confidential. A movement can then
accept a patron's support without adopting the patron's aims.

## What the framework doesn't claim

The Social Charter sets out to coordinate, and it leaves several questions to
others:

- It isn't a theory of justice. It doesn't decide what the good is. It judges
  a state by its members' welfare, which each member weighs by their own will.
- It doesn't prescribe how each charter is designed. Where a choice already
  has a known range of options, such as how a charter can change its rules and
  still resist capture, each charter makes that choice.

The question this project started from, whether ordinary people can still
change the order they live under, remains open. The Social Charter proposes
how they might: by acting together, across the places where leverage remains,
while the openings last.
