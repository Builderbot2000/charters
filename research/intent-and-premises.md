# Intent, thesis, and premises of the Social Charter

This document gathers the Social Charter's intent, thesis, and premises in one
place. It then tests whether each premise holds. It draws on the Intent and
Premise sections of the glossary, the author's statements from September 26
to October 22, 2026, and the
[literature review](literature-review.md).

Status as of October 28, 2026. The author answered the last framework-level
decisions on that day. The answers, and a restated thesis, are recorded in
[Answers on October 28, 2026](#answers-on-october-28-2026) and integrated into
the glossary. Earlier sections keep the October 22 analysis as it was.

## Consolidated statement

The following statement uses only positions the author has already taken.

### Motivating question

What is the best configuration of a society whose members each define "best"
differently?

### Problem

Divided interests over the path forward keep the status quo in place, so
nothing gets done. Modern democracies show this problem clearly.

### Factional interest

Common interest has always formed factions inside existing groups. Factional
interest comes in two kinds, and each kind has two effects at once:

| Kind of factional interest | Constructive effect | Destructive effect |
|---|---|---|
| Positive: an idealistic difference of direction | Develops the group's future states by exploring approaches nobody had considered | Subverts the group's intended purpose as the faction uses its influence |
| Negative: a pragmatic difference of material position and gains | Protects member rights by giving members an influential voice | Ossifies the group's development through entrenched positions |

Left alone, these interests drift to whatever equilibrium their struggle
produces. That equilibrium can work against the common good, as a negative-sum
situation or a market for lemons. The Social Charter provides a universal
framework that mediates between these interests, so that their behavior can be
engineered from above toward positive-sum results.

### Strategy

Big tents are the most reliable path to impactful change, because united
coalitions have more resources and represent more people than single-purpose
organizations. This advantage holds only if the coalition is coordinated
effectively. The big tent is a strategy, not the purpose.

### Thesis

The Social Charter supplies the coordination that big tents need. It's an
optimization framework that delineates overlapping interests explicitly. Each
meeting of interests then becomes a constrained, known game-theoretic problem,
and members can install a mechanism designed for that problem instead of
relying on implicit consensus or a network of bilateral deals. The framework is
a common blueprint for institution design, with a calculable path toward
better states.

### Scope and boundaries

The framework sets these boundaries:

- A conflict is either arbitrated by a parent charter whose common interest
  covers it, or settled where it is by bounded noncooperative struggle.
- The highest human charter is the largest coalition that shares a real
  interest, not all of humanity.
- That coalition decides whom it excludes. No principled exclusion rule
  constrains it.
- The framework judges states by members' welfare, which is each member's real
  freedom to act on their own will.

### Claim of advantage

The Social Charter reaches better states more efficiently than traditional
democratic or autocratic structures.

## Premises and hidden assumptions

The glossary states four premises. The thesis also depends on six assumptions
that the glossary doesn't state as premises.

| ID | Statement | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Decentralized movements can't form and enact a clear agenda (question of arbitration). | Stated |
| P2 | Centralized movements suffer from the vanguard's own interests (vanguard interest). | Stated |
| P3 | Implicit consensus through side channels settles into worse outcomes because positions stay hidden (obscured consensus). | Stated |
| P4 | Some conflicts have no shared interest above them, and some groups are too alien to include (limit of arbitration). | Stated |
| A1 | Scale makes coalitions more effective, if they're coordinated. | Hidden |
| A2 | Interests can be separated into distinct charters. | Hidden |
| A3 | Members can state their positions. | Hidden |
| A4 | A specified meeting of interests is calculable: its game is known and a suitable mechanism can be found. | Hidden |
| A5 | Charter outcomes bind members who would prefer to defect. | Hidden |
| A6 | Much of the status quo's persistence is hidden agreement, not real disagreement. | Hidden |

## Assessment of each premise

Each verdict uses one of three ratings:

- **Holds**: well supported, and the framework uses it correctly.
- **Holds if narrowed**: true in a narrower form than the glossary states.
- **At risk**: contested, or in tension with another part of the framework.

### P1. Question of arbitration: holds

Freeman's "The Tyranny of Structurelessness" (1972), Tufekci's *Twitter and
Tear Gas* (2017), and Tsebelis's work on veto players support the diagnosis.

One caution applies. The glossary entry claims that much deadlock comes from
failures of discovery, commitment, and cost, not from a lack of possible
agreement. Tsebelis attributes gridlock mainly to the distance between veto
players' positions, which is real disagreement. That distinction is A6.

### P2. Vanguard interest: holds as a diagnosis

Michels's iron law of oligarchy (1911) and principal–agent theory support the
diagnosis. The remedy is less settled. Replacing discretion with mechanisms
doesn't remove the vanguard. It moves the vanguard to whoever designs the
mechanism, writes the objective function, runs the audit, or interprets the
charter. The glossary already lists interpreter capture as an open problem.
The same risk applies to mechanism designers.

P1 and P2 together form a dilemma: decentralized structures can't act, and
centralized structures serve their leaders. The Social Charter claims a third
option, in which members own the objective and a mechanism executes it. That
claim is the framework's central answer to both premises. It needs its own
argument, and it inherits the multitask problem described in the literature
review (Holmström and Milgrom, 1991).

### P3. Obscured consensus: holds if narrowed

The literature on transparency in negotiation (Prat, 2005; Stasavage, 2007)
finds that public positions can harden and that closed negotiation can help
compromise. Agents also misreport their positions to mechanisms
(Gibbard–Satterthwaite).

The premise survives if it separates two kinds of transparency:

- Transparency of the problem: who the players are, what's in scope, which
  rules apply, and which outcomes are possible
- Transparency of positions: each party's stated preferences during bargaining

The framework needs the first kind. It doesn't need the second, because a
mechanism can take sealed or confidential inputs, as a sealed-bid auction
does. The SEC analogy fits the first kind, which is mandatory disclosure of
the rules and material facts, not of every trader's intentions. Narrowed this
way, P3 avoids the strongest objection.

### P4. Limit of arbitration: holds

This premise is a realistic observation, and it gives the framework an honest
boundary. Two weaknesses remain:

- Coalition-decided exclusion is circular: the coalition has to exist before
  it can decide who belongs. This circularity is the democratic boundary
  problem (Whelan, 1983). The author accepts it as realism, which is
  defensible, but the glossary needs to state that it describes what happens,
  not what's justified.
- The example of a tribe that hunts people is an easy case. Real exclusion
  disputes, such as over migrants or rival states, are contested. A coalition
  could exclude a weak minority to take its share, and fundamental protections
  apply only inside the coalition.

### A1. Scale makes coalitions effective: at risk

Olson (1965, 1982) shows that large, diffuse groups organize worse than small,
concentrated ones. The framework's answer is that unbundling lowers the cost
of holding a coalition together.

That answer creates an internal tension. A conventional big tent holds
together through logrolling: each faction accepts the others' issues to get
its own. The Social Charter unbundles issues, so each charter becomes a broad
membership around one narrow interest. That structure resembles a
single-purpose organization with many members, which is the kind of
organization the big-tent strategy claims to outperform. The scale advantage
then has to come from somewhere else, most likely from parent charters that
pool the resources of their children. The glossary doesn't yet explain how
resources and leverage pool across charters.

### A2. Interests are separable: at risk

When a preference on one issue depends on another, voting issue by issue can
produce an outcome that almost nobody wants (Brams, Kilgour, and Zwicker,
1998). Drawing the boundary of each charter is also a political act, much like
drawing electoral districts. The framework needs a rule, or at least a
mechanism, for deciding where one interest ends and another begins.

### A3. Members can state positions: holds if narrowed

Converse (1964) and Achen and Bartels (2016) find that most citizens lack
stable issue positions. The framework doesn't need every member to hold a
position on every question, however. It needs members to hold a position on
the interest that brought them into a charter, and it can also use state
signals and revealed preference. The glossary needs to say this explicitly.

### A4. Meetings of interests are calculable: holds as a design objective

The author calls this assumption the most important part of the thesis. It
holds in a weak form, but not in the strong form the glossary uses.

The strong form says that each meeting is a known game, and that the framework
can calculate which mechanism fits and whether the core is empty. Several
results work against it:

- Mechanism design assumes that the designer knows the distribution of
  players' private types. In politics, that distribution is rarely known.
- Testing whether the core is empty needs the value of every subcoalition. In
  politics, those values are unknown, and computing the core is hard even when
  they're known (Deng and Papadimitriou, 1994).
- The market-design successes that the glossary cites, kidney exchange,
  residency matching, and spectrum auctions, all have well-defined goods, a
  designer with enforcement power, and repeated rounds that allow tuning.
  Most meetings of interests lack at least one of these.
- Impossibility results, such as Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, and
  Myerson–Satterthwaite, guarantee that some meetings have no mechanism with
  every desirable property.

The weak form says that each meeting is specified in a standard way, so
members can see the structure of the problem, choose from known mechanisms,
see which trade-offs they're making, and tune the mechanism over time. This
form is defensible, and it's what Ostrom's IAD framework does descriptively.
It also matches the glossary's own statement that a defined problem isn't
always a solvable one. The phrase "calculable path" overstates it. A phrase
such as "a specified path" or "an engineering path" fits the weak form.

**Author's position:** Calculability means the availability of signal, and
producing that signal is a task for each charter's mechanism design. The
deliberative process during execution is the game. Each charter engineers its
incentives to give every party the strongest available incentive to express
its position truthfully.

**Reassessment:** This position answers the first objection. The designer
doesn't need to know players' types in advance, because the mechanism elicits
them. This position is the revelation principle used as a design goal. It
also covers A3, because a charter elicits positions instead of assuming them.

The word "maximum" matters. Truthfulness is a constrained optimum, not a
guarantee:

- Gibbard–Satterthwaite rules out a deterministic, strategy-proof,
  non-dictatorial mechanism over three or more outcomes when preferences are
  unrestricted. Charters escape this result through restricted domains, such
  as single-peaked preferences, through monetary or resource transfers, as
  Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanisms do, or through randomization.
- VCG mechanisms make truth-telling optimal, but they don't balance their
  budgets, and groups of parties can collude against them. A charter needs
  incentives that resist collusion as well as individual misreporting.
- Myerson–Satterthwaite still applies. A charter can't have truthfulness,
  voluntary participation, efficiency, and a balanced budget all at once, so
  each charter chooses which property to give up.

With truthful signals, a charter can estimate whether a coalition can hold
together. It can't compute that answer exactly. The thesis holds if it
promises a design method that pushes toward truthful signals, not a
calculation.

### A5. Outcomes bind members: holds if penalties are collectible

The question of arbitration lists commitment as one of three failures behind
deadlock. Acemoglu (2003) argues that commitment is the fundamental reason
political bargains fail. The Social Charter treats charters as voluntary
associations with exit. A member that loses an arbitration can leave, which
reintroduces the commitment problem the framework claims to solve.

The glossary doesn't yet say what makes an arbitration binding: exit costs,
bonds, enforcement by a parent, or reputation. This gap is the largest one
the review found, because P1, A1, and the claim of advantage all depend on
binding outcomes.

**Author's position:** Outcomes bind despite exit. Before arbitration
begins, the parties agree on the conditions and penalties of exit, so exit
is a calculated scenario.

**Reassessment:** This position is a standard commitment device, much like a
performance bond or a liquidated-damages clause. It closes the gap in
principle. Three conditions decide whether it works in practice:

- **Collectibility:** a party that has exited is outside the charter, so the
  charter can't collect a penalty from it unless the party posts collateral
  before arbitration or a parent charter enforces the penalty. At the highest
  human charter, no parent exists, so collateral or reputation is the only
  option.
- **Sizing:** a party exits whenever its loss from the outcome exceeds the
  penalty. Some of these exits are efficient, so the penalty sets how binding
  each arbitration is. The penalty is a design parameter, not a fixed rule.
- **Participation:** parties that expect heavy penalties might refuse to
  enter arbitration. A refusal sends the conflict to bounded struggle, which
  is consistent with the framework, but it limits how much arbitration
  covers.

### A6. Deadlock contains reconcilable agreement: holds as a narrower claim

The claim of advantage depends on how much of the status quo's persistence
comes from hidden agreement that the framework can unlock. Where real
disagreement causes deadlock, the framework sends the conflict to bounded
struggle, which resembles what happens now.

Most consequential political change also redistributes, so some parties lose.
The cooperative mode handles changes that make everyone better off, or changes
that a parent arbitrates. Combined with exit (A5), the framework might handle
mainly the changes that were already easy, and leave the redistributive ones
to struggle. If so, the framework doesn't solve the problem it targets. This
possibility is empirical and doesn't refute the thesis, but the thesis needs
to address it.

**Author's position:** A deadlock isn't necessarily complete disagreement. It
is a common state that probably holds some degree of reconcilability, and the
charter structure extracts that reconcilability efficiently. When the
reconcilable agreement is used up, a dissolution clause lets members stop
maintaining a charter that can no longer combine agreements to gain
influence through scale.

**Reassessment:** This position replaces the claim that deadlock is mostly
hidden agreement with a narrower one: most deadlocks hold some reconcilable
agreement, and charters extract it faster than current institutions do. The
narrower claim is more defensible. It also gives each charter a test that
can fail. If a charter stops producing agreement, members dissolve it, which
keeps failed coalitions from lasting.

Two points remain:

- The claim of advantage now rests on how efficiently charters extract
  agreement, compared with legislatures and informal bargaining. That
  comparison is still untested.
- Dissolution needs its own safeguards. A party that can trigger dissolution
  can threaten it to win concessions, which is the holdout problem. The
  dissolution clause needs a threshold and conditions, as exit does.

## Assessment of the factional intent

The author added this intent on October 22, 2026, after the first review.

### Support from the literature

The factional intent restates one of the oldest problems in political design,
and each of its four effects has support:

- **Factions arise from common interest:** Madison, *Federalist No. 10*
  (1787), argues that the causes of faction can't be removed, only their
  effects controlled. The Social Charter takes the same position.
- **Exploration by idealistic factions:** research on minority influence
  (Moscovici, 1976) and on dissent in groups (Nemeth) finds that dissenting
  minorities improve a group's search for options, even when they lose.
- **Subversion of purpose:** Merton's *goal displacement* (1940) and
  Selznick's *co-optation* in *TVA and the Grass Roots* (1949) describe how a
  faction redirects an organization toward its own ends.
- **Representation by material factions:** pluralists such as Truman (1951)
  and Dahl (1961) treat interest groups as the channel through which members'
  claims reach decisions.
- **Ossification by material factions:** Olson's *The Rise and Decline of
  Nations* (1982) argues that accumulating distributional coalitions slow
  adaptation and growth.
- **Drift to a bad equilibrium:** Tullock's work on rent-seeking (1967) shows
  how competition for transfers wastes resources, which is a negative-sum
  result.

The market-for-lemons analogy fits better than it first appears. In Akerlof's
model (1970), hidden information drives high-quality sellers out of a market.
In a factional group, Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970) predicts a
similar pattern: the members most committed to the group's purpose leave
first, and the members seeking material gain stay. This link also ties the
factional intent to obscured consensus.

### How the intent fits the thesis

The factional intent gives the thesis a deeper root. A meeting of interests
is, in most cases, a meeting of factions, and the charter's mechanism is the
mediation that the intent calls for.

The intent also suggests an answer to the big-tent problem in A1. A faction
inside a big tent could form a child charter instead of splitting the tent.
Sibling charters would then explore different directions, which is the
constructive effect of positive factions, while the parent keeps the scale.
This answer is a possible link, not a position the author has taken.

### Issues to resolve

The intent raises four issues:

1. **Motive or effect.** Motives are hidden, and most real factions mix
   ideals with material interest. A mechanism can't sort factions by motive.
   It can respond to observable behavior, such as proposals, votes, transfers,
   and exits. The two-by-two table holds as an analysis. As a design target,
   the four effects are more usable than the two kinds.
1. **Constructive and destructive effects share one cause.** The same
   behavior that explores new directions can also subvert the group's
   purpose. The same voice that protects rights can also entrench positions.
   Suppressing the behavior removes both effects, so a mechanism has to
   channel it instead. Exploration could go to sibling charters, and
   representation could have a defined voice with sunset or review terms.
   The generativity gap open problem already covers part of this issue.
1. **"Engineered from above" and the vanguard interest.** The phrase can read
   as a return to central control, which P2 rejects. The intended meaning is
   probably that the parent charter's mechanism, which members choose, does
   the engineering. The wording needs to say so.
1. **"The common good" and neutrality.** Positive-sum results need a measure,
   and the framework avoids a single idea of the good. Defining the common
   good as the common interest that each charter states keeps the framework
   neutral. That definition also ties this issue to the undefined measure in
   [Internal tensions](#internal-tensions).

### Precedents to build on

No single work combines the four effects with a design method, but these
bodies of work cover the parts:

| Part of the factional intent | Building block |
|---|---|
| Two kinds of faction | Hume, "Of Parties in General" (1742): factions of interest, principle, and affection. Sartori, *Parties and Party Systems* (1976). |
| Each kind has constructive and destructive effects | Coser, *The Functions of Social Conflict* (1956), building on Simmel |
| Incentives decide which effect appears | Boucek, "Rethinking Factionalism" (2009) and *Factional Politics* (2012): cooperative, competitive, and degenerative factionalism |
| Steering toward positive-sum results | Olson, *The Rise and Decline of Nations* (1982): distributional coalitions and encompassing organizations |
| Exploration by idealistic factions | March, "Exploration and Exploitation in Organizational Learning" (1991). Cyert and March, *A Behavioral Theory of the Firm* (1963). |
| Representation and entrenchment by material factions | Grossman and Helpman, *Special Interest Politics* (2001) |
| Evidence on when internal conflict helps | Jehn (1995). De Dreu and Weingart (2003). de Wit, Greer, and Jehn (2012). |
| Rival view: no central design needed | Lindblom, *The Intelligence of Democracy* (1965) |

A defensible framing combines Hume's and Sartori's typology, Coser's dual
functions, Boucek's claim that incentives decide the outcome, and Olson's
encompassing interest. The Social Charter's contribution is a general
mechanism for steering factional behavior at every level of a recursive
structure.

### Conditions from the literature

Each building block makes claims that limit how the factional rationale can
be applied. The following conditions are ordered by how much they threaten
the rationale.

1. **Charters can become distributional coalitions (Olson).** Every charter
   serves a narrow interest compared with its parent. A system that makes
   charters cheap to form might multiply the coalitions that Olson blames for
   stagnation. The rationale holds only if each parent is encompassing
   compared with its children, and if the parent can arbitrate the
   rent-seeking of its children. Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action*
   (1965) adds a second condition: large groups form only with selective
   incentives or low organizing costs, so the charter system has to provide
   one of them.

   **Author's position:** A charter represents the common interest among
   narrower interests by gathering the largest bloc that shares it. A charter
   can address a single issue, but single issues at higher levels carry great
   weight, such as preserving a planetary ecosystem compared with one city's
   yearly output. A charter exists to define a commons on which codified
   deliberation and rulings operate.

   **Reassessment:** Mostly answered. Olson measures how encompassing a group
   is by the share of its costs that it bears, not by how many issues it
   covers, so a single-issue charter can be encompassing. For narrower
   charters, the externality and coordination principle entries already send
   costs imposed on outsiders to a parent that covers both sides, which is
   Olson's remedy. One gap remains: the externality entry relies on affected
   parties to escalate, and Olson predicts that people who bear diffuse costs
   are the least likely to organize. Whether a parent waits for escalation,
   acts on its own initiative, or acts through an audit is left to the design
   of each charter.
1. **Factions of principle resist bargaining (Hume, Tetlock).** Hume ranks
   factions of principle as more dangerous than factions of interest, which
   reverses the "positive" and "negative" labels. Research on sacred values
   (Tetlock, 2003; Atran and Axelrod, 2008) finds that people refuse to trade
   such values and react with outrage when offered compensation. Mechanisms
   that rely on transfers or trade-offs fail for these factions. Factions of
   principle need a channel other than bargaining, such as forming a sibling
   charter, amendment, or exit. Replacing "positive" and "negative" with
   "principle" and "interest" also removes a judgment the literature doesn't
   support.

   **Author's position:** A faction of principle has no third option. It
   either gains influence over the whole commons, and so becomes the higher
   charter, or it stays unrepresented and remains subject to the charter
   without a voice.

   **Reassessment:** Consistent with the framework, with two qualifications.
   First, research on devoted actors (Atran) finds that people defending
   sacred values accept heavy costs before they accept a deal, so in practice
   these factions fall into bounded struggle under the limit of arbitration.
   The framework contains them but doesn't absorb them. Second, Atran and
   Axelrod (2008) found that symbolic concessions, such as recognition, can
   move holders of sacred values when material offers can't. Charters could
   offer factions of principle a channel based on recognition.

   The position also exposes a gap. The jurisdiction entry gives a charter
   authority over people, but fundamental protections cover only members, and
   no entry says how a charter treats non-members inside its commons, such as
   refugees. The literature treats rule without voice as a problem: Walzer
   (*Spheres of Justice*, 1983) calls permanent rule over metics a form of
   tyranny, and the all-subjected principle (Abizadeh, 2008) holds that
   anyone a rule coerces is owed a say in it. Precedents for a clause include
   Hammar's denizenship (1990) and non-refoulement under the Refugee
   Convention. The options are no protection beyond ordinary rules, basic
   protection without voice, or basic protection with a path to membership.
   This choice has an established range of options, so it's left to the
   design of each charter. The framework only needs to state that every
   charter makes it.
1. **Measurement suppresses exploration (March).** Exploration has
   uncertain, distant, and often negative returns. Adaptive processes favor
   exploitation, so organizations fall into competency traps (Levitt and
   March, 1988). A charter that optimizes a measured objective will tend to
   suppress its idealistic factions. The rationale needs a protected place
   for exploration, such as a budget for sibling experiments. The generativity
   gap open problem covers part of this condition.

   **Author's position:** Loose measurement leaves room for innovative
   approaches. The more loosely a charter defines an article, the more that
   article is open to change, whether good or bad. Each charter sets this
   looseness to fit its purpose. For example, a research charter might
   measure loosely, while a transportation charter might be tightly
   regulated.

   **Reassessment:** Answered. Measurement tightness is a design dial with a
   clear range, so each charter sets it. This answer matches March's
   trade-off: loose measurement favors exploration, and tight measurement
   favors exploitation. The framework only needs to state that the dial
   exists and that it trades exploration against drift from purpose.
1. **A faction's face changes over time (Boucek).** Boucek argues that fixed
   typologies explain little, because the same faction moves between
   cooperative, competitive, and degenerative behavior as incentives change.
   She also finds that long, secure dominance breeds degenerative
   factionalism. A charter can't classify a faction once. It has to watch
   behavior continuously, through audits, and keep incumbents contestable,
   through rotation, sunset terms, or competing siblings.
1. **Paying for truthfulness can legalize bought influence (Grossman and
   Helpman).** Interest groups both inform decision-makers and buy influence.
   A4 suggests transfers as one way to get truthful signals, but transfers
   also open a channel for buying outcomes. The charter has to separate the
   information channel, through verified signals, from the payment channel.
1. **Vague goals can hold coalitions together (Cyert and March).** Real
   coalitions often work because their goals stay vague, are dealt with one
   at a time, and are cushioned by slack resources. Making every objective
   explicit might remove that cushion. This condition matches the
   transparency objection to obscured consensus (P3). Charters might need to
   allow slack and to resolve goals in sequence.

   **Author's position:** A charter's fundamental ingredients are its commons
   and its mechanisms for deliberation, self-modification, and execution.
   Where a real commons exists, such as shared resources or property, clear
   decisions have to be made. Symbolism can be added to any charter, but it's
   cosmetic. Where no commons exists, there's no need for a charter.

   **Reassessment:** Answered. Cyert and March describe decisions about
   concrete resources, such as budgets and allocations, that are made clearly
   while members' broader goals stay vague. The author's position needs
   clarity only about the commons and the mechanism, not about every member's
   reasons. This position supports narrowing P3 to transparency of the
   problem. It also places the recognition channel from condition 2 as an
   optional, cosmetic addition, not a substitute for decisions about the
   commons. Slack and the order in which goals are addressed are left to
   charter design.
1. **Conflict helps only under conditions (Jehn; De Dreu and Weingart).**
   Disagreement about the task improves results only when relationships stay
   intact, trust is high, and the task isn't routine. Disagreement about the
   task also tends to turn into personal conflict. The rationale can claim a
   conditional benefit, not a general one, and mechanisms need to keep
   disagreement about proposals apart from conflict between people, for
   example through anonymous proposals or structured dissent.

   **Author's position:** The higher charter's design and executive
   authority mediate against destructive conflict and establish trust. Trust
   is part of the metrics that the mechanism optimizes for.

   **Reassessment:** Answered. Making trust an objective of the parent's
   mechanism meets the condition the research identifies, which is that
   disagreement helps only when trust is high. How a parent measures trust
   and keeps disagreement about proposals apart from conflict between people
   is left to charter design.
1. **Some factions are about loyalty, not direction or gains (Hume, Coser).**
   Hume's third kind, factions of affection, forms around people. Coser's
   nonrealistic conflict releases tension and has no outcome that would
   satisfy it. Neither fits the two-kind model, and no outcome mechanism
   resolves either one. Factions of affection are close to the vanguard
   interest, so P2 might cover them.

   **Author's position and reassessment:** Answered. Whether a charter
   permits factions of affection is left to its clauses and design. Where
   such a faction captures leadership, the vanguard interest (P2) already
   applies.
1. **Factions coordinate without a designer (Lindblom).** Partisan mutual
   adjustment coordinates factions without central design. Lindblom's later
   *Politics and Markets* (1977) concedes that this adjustment favors groups
   with more resources, especially business. The rationale is stronger if it
   claims to structure and correct mutual adjustment, not replace it. That
   framing also answers the concern that "engineered from above" conflicts
   with the vanguard interest.

   **Author's position and reassessment:** Answered on October 23, 2026, by
   the scope rule. The framework states that charters structure and correct
   mutual adjustment instead of replacing it. How a charter corrects the bias
   toward groups with more resources, for example through matching funds,
   quadratic mechanisms, or agenda rules, is left to mechanism design.
1. **Suppressing faction destroys liberty (Madison).** *Federalist No. 10*
   rejects removing the causes of faction, and *Federalist No. 51* relies on
   ambition counteracting ambition. Madison's remedy is many competing
   factions, which the charter graph already resembles. Mechanisms can shape
   what factions do, but they mustn't restrict forming them.

   **Author's position and reassessment:** Answered on October 23, 2026, by
   the scope rule. The framework states the limit. Each charter's clauses
   carry it out, drawing on constitutional practice for freedom of
   association and for checks and balances.
1. **Subversion can be legitimate.** A group's intended purpose can be wrong,
   and today's subversion can become tomorrow's purpose. The rationale needs
   a test that separates the two. One option is to treat a change of purpose
   through amendment as legitimate, and a change of purpose through capture,
   without amendment, as subversion.

   **Author's position and reassessment:** Answered on October 23, 2026, by
   the scope rule. The framework states the distinction between amendment and
   capture. Each charter's amendment clause sets the thresholds, drawing on
   established practice such as supermajorities, entrenchment clauses, and
   the doctrine of unconstitutional amendments.

## Internal tensions

The following tensions come from parts of the framework that pull against each
other, not from outside literature:

1. **Big tent and unbundling.** Unbundling removes the logrolling that holds
   conventional big tents together. See A1.
1. **Voluntary exit and commitment.** Exit protects members but weakens
   binding outcomes. Exit penalties agreed before arbitration resolve this
   tension if the penalties are collectible. See A5.
1. **Cooperative resolution and the status quo.** The cooperative mode
   favors changes that everyone accepts, while the problem the framework
   targets is change that some parties resist. The author's narrower claim
   accepts this limit: charters extract whatever agreement exists and
   dissolve when none is left. See A6.
1. **Will-weighted welfare and Arrow.** The Social Charter entry cites Arrow,
   but a welfare measure that rules out comparison between people keeps
   Arrow's conditions in force. Sen (1970) showed that allowing comparison
   escapes them. See the literature review.
1. **Neutrality and the claim of advantage.** "Better states" and "more
   efficiently" need a measure. Will-weighted welfare is that measure, but it
   isn't yet defined for whole states, only for each member.

## Overall verdict

The diagnostic premises hold. P1, P2, and P4 are well supported, and P3 holds
once it separates transparency of the problem from transparency of positions.

The weakest part of the framework is the step from diagnosis to remedy. The
thesis depends on A4, A5, and A6, which the glossary doesn't state as
premises. The author's positions from October 22, 2026, answer the main
objection to each:

- A4 holds as a design objective: charters engineer incentives toward
  truthful signals, within known impossibility limits.
- A5 holds if exit penalties are agreed before arbitration and are
  collectible.
- A6 holds as a narrower claim: charters extract the reconcilable agreement
  that a deadlock contains, and dissolve when it's used up.

The largest remaining problem is A1: unbundling removes the logrolling that
holds conventional big tents together, and the glossary doesn't yet say how
scale advantages pool across charters.

A defensible restatement of the thesis is as follows: the Social Charter
specifies every meeting of interests in a standard form, so that members can
choose and tune mechanisms with the trade-offs in view, and so that broad
coalitions can coordinate around shared interests without first agreeing on
everything else. This restatement keeps the core idea and drops the claims
the literature contradicts.

## Answers on October 28, 2026

The author answered or delegated every remaining framework-level decision,
and restated the thesis.

### Decisions delegated or already closed

Five of the seven decisions turned out to be charter design, or were already
answered by the glossary's wording:

- **Decision 1, narrowing obscured consensus.** Delegated. Whether positions
  are public or sealed is a choice of mechanism. The framework needs only
  transparency of the problem, which the charter model supplies. The
  obscured consensus entry now says so.
- **Decision 2, factions as child charters.** Delegated. Whether a faction
  becomes a child charter, a tier, or a separate charter is a choice of
  centralization, which is a charter parameter.
- **Decision 3, who engineers "from above."** Closed. The author routed it to
  constitutional design on October 24, 2026, and on October 28 made democracy
  a design choice. Whoever holds constitutional authority does the
  engineering.
- **Decision 4, the meaning of "the common good."** Closed. The factional
  interest entry already uses each group's common interest.
- **Decision 6, coalition-decided exclusion.** Delegated. Exclusion is the
  assignment of a status, which each charter makes. The framework doesn't
  claim that exclusion is legitimate, because it's infrastructure, not a
  moral theory.

### Decision 5, stating dependencies

The author's position: the framework's dependencies are recorded in its
premises. A2, separability of interests, is folded into a new premise. Where
one person holds interests that depend on each other, such as keeping money
and investing it to earn more, the person arbitrates among them as their own
personal corporation. At some level, interests are atomic and independent.
Dependencies between charters are spillovers that a common parent handles.

### Decision 7 and the restated thesis

The author called the claim of advantage over democratic and autocratic
structures an outdated artifact from when the framework was more
opinionated. The restated thesis, in the author's words:

> The charter provides a universal framework that promotes maximal
> organizational efficiency at any scale by allowing the targeted application
> of well-studied theoretical heuristics onto the model of a known problem at
> any point of the societal fabric, under the assumption that the local
> optimums are greedily scalable to the global optima because of the theory
> around efficiency of informational adjacency and granularity of control.
> This is now a framework that is infrastructure to the opinionated ideals and
> societal conditions that will serve as its parameters to form an optimal
> ideology.

The thesis rests on a new assumption: local optima scale to the global
optimum. Greedy optimization doesn't scale in general, as the prisoner's
dilemma and the question of arbitration show. The author accepted a narrowed
form: local optima add up to the global optimum when society is nearly
decomposable (Simon, 1962) and parent charters internalize the spillovers
between their children. Hayek (1945) supports informational adjacency, Ashby's
law of requisite variety supports granularity of control, and Oates (1972)
states the no-spillover condition.

The author wants a later study that fits a charter's design to a known
problem and verifies that the structure scales to an optimum.

### Four parts

The author divided the project into four parts:

- **Framework.** The generic structure that every charter shares. This part
  is the existing glossary.
- **Model.** Observations of a specific society as it is.
- **Question.** What a specific society ought to be.
- **Answer.** What is to be done in a specific scenario.

Only the Framework is generic. The other three parts apply it to specific
scenarios, so generic content stays in the Framework even when it carries an
opinion. For example, the big tent is a generic justification of merit, so it
stays. The author hasn't started work on the other three parts, and they're
empty.

The welfare entry stays in the Framework as a generic measure that a charter
can adopt in its objective function. It's no longer the Framework's own
measure. The claim of advantage over democratic and autocratic structures is
dropped, because the restated thesis replaces it.

### Effect on the glossary

- **Social Charter.** Restated around the thesis. Welfare is no longer the
  framework's measure, and the claim of advantage is removed.
- **Decomposability.** New premise entry, in the narrowed form, with A2
  folded in.
- **Welfare.** Moved from the Intent section to the Execution section, as a
  measure that a charter can adopt.
- **Obscured consensus.** States transparency of the problem, and leaves the
  transparency of positions to each mechanism.
- **Site structure.** The page now groups its sections into the four parts.
  The Model, Question, and Answer parts are empty.
